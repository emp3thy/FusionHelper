# Wine bottle and rack-slot dimensions: research report

Date: 2026-10-03. All lengths in mm unless stated; inch figures from US sources are converted at 25.4 mm/in. Where a figure comes from a manufacturer data sheet it is marked (DS); where it comes from a hobbyist's calliper measurements of real bottles it is marked (field); where it comes from a retailer/guide it is marked (guide).

Caveat on access: several sites (VintageView help centre, Vintage Cellars, Vigilant, IKEA.com, Google Patents, Jancis Robinson, Wine Spectator, Vinotemp) refused automated fetching. Figures from those sites are taken from search-result snippets and are flagged "(snippet)". Saverglass PDF data sheets and the VintageView spec PDF were downloaded and text-extracted directly.

### 1.6 Common items

- 750 ml is the legal/standard still-wine volume in the EU, US and most markets; the standard Champagne "bouteille" is 75 cl [C4].
- Bore (inside neck at mouth) is 18.5 mm by EN 12726 / CETIE GME 50.1 for cork finishes, widening to 21 mm below the mouth [N1][WP1]. Champagne crown finishes use a 29 mm crown (NF H 35-029) [N2].
- Neck/finish outer diameter: ~28–29.5 mm on still-wine cork finishes (Saverglass drawings) [S3][S5]; 29 mm crown on Champagne [S8][N2].
- Overall "standard 750 ml" envelope used by rack makers: 292–330 tall × 73–89 dia, with 305 × 76–81 as the design nominal [W1][D1].

### 2.3 Depth and protrusion

- Full-enclosure depth for a 305 (12") bottle: 368 (14-1/2") [D1]; Vigilant cubbies are 368 deep [VG1].
- Typical US commercial wood racking is 314–343 (12-3/8"–13-1/2") deep [W4][R1][W3], so a 320–330 Bordeaux protrudes 0–15 mm and a 353 flûte protrudes ~10–40 mm. Label-forward VintageView presentation rows are 330 (13") deep [V1].
- 12"-deep home racks with 12"+ bottles are normal; "as long as the bulk of the bottle is resting on the rack, you're OK ... most racks allow the bottles to stick out so you can grab them" [WB1].
- VintageView single-bottle-depth wall racks are only 133 (5-1/4") deep; the bottle (axis parallel to the wall) stands proud of the rack by roughly half its diameter [V1].

## 3. Storage guidance: orientation, tilt, support geometry

### 3.1 Horizontal vs angled

- Horizontal (bottle axis level) is the default for cork-closed wine so the wine keeps the cork wet; "most wine racks are designed for horizontal storage" [WP2][R2].
- A slight tilt is acceptable and is used deliberately by some rack designs: neck slightly elevated, no more than ~15°, keeps the cork mostly wetted, discourages bottles sliding out in a disturbance, and settles sediment toward the base corner [WP2 snippet]. Late-1990s research cited by Wikipedia argues a slight angle (ullage bubble at the top of the bottle rather than mid-cork) is marginally better than dead level [WP2].
- Rosehill: "horizontally or on a 45° angle with the cork facing down" [R2]. Patent prior art describes neck-down racks of 20–45° [P1 snippet]. Neck-down angles greater than ~15° are a display/novelty choice, not a storage requirement.
- Sparkling wine: Wikipedia notes studies that Champagne ages faster on its side (cork loses elasticity in contact with wine) and that upright is acceptable; the Champagne industry nevertheless recommends on-side storage at ~10 °C [WP2]. Screw-cap and fortified wines: any orientation / upright [R2].

Design recommendation derived from the above: build the rack level (0°) or with 2–5° neck-up; never neck-down for storage.

### 3.2 Support geometry and why bottles roll or slip

- Two-rail cradle (the dominant wood-rack design): bottle rests in the gap between two parallel rails spaced less than the bottle diameter (e.g. 70 mm between 19 mm rails at 89 mm pitch [F1]). Each rail touches the cylinder along a line; the two contact lines form a V that resists rolling. The narrower the gap relative to diameter, the shallower the V and the less lateral restraint; the wider the gap, the lower the bottle sits and the more it "locks" in (but a Bordeaux 76 dia must not fall through a gap meant for 90 dia Champagne).
- Front/rear support spacing: US patent 9,289,062 states that the spacing between the forward and rearward supports must be such that the bottle's centre of gravity lies between them, and that the forward cut-out should be smaller so the shoulder taper lands on it and the bottle cannot slide forward [P2 snippet]. For a 320 mm Bordeaux, a full bottle's CoG is roughly 40–45 % of the overall length from the base (~130–145 mm), because the liquid fills the ~180 mm cylindrical body and the empty neck is light; place the rear support within ~40–60 mm of the base and the front support under the body/shoulder transition (~190–230 mm from the base) so the CoG sits well inside the span. A front support beyond the shoulder (under the neck) carries no body contact and lets the bottle tip.
- Neck-ring / cantilever supports (VintageView style, patents 7,195,125 / 7,080,743): a ring engages the neck at four points (two lower-front, two upper-rear) so the bottle hangs cantilevered; no body support needed, but the ring bore must suit the 28–30 mm neck and the bottle shoulder must clear [P3 snippet]. VintageView's 102 mm vertical pitch accommodates 89–95 dia bottles [V1].
- Scalloped / V-groove shelves: a scallop cut to the bottle radius gives arc contact (good for a single diameter, loose for others); a V-groove gives two-line contact for any diameter, which is why V and two-rail cradles are preferred for mixed collections.
- Why bottles roll: a cylinder on a flat shelf is neutrally stable; any shelf slope or bump lets it roll. Why bottles slip axially: on a level two-rail cradle the only axial restraint is friction. A neck-up tilt pushes the base against the back; a neck-down tilt lets a straight-sided Bordeaux slide forward until the shoulder catches a front rail or ring — so the front support must be positioned to catch the shoulder, or the rack must be level/neck-up. Thick-glass Champagne and Burgundy bottles have long sloping shoulders, so they slide further before catching [P2].
- Earthquake / knock protection: a small neck-up angle plus a front lip or ring is the standard answer [WP2].
- Minimum support length: there is no published standard; practice (two-rail racks 300–343 deep, VintageView rings on the neck) shows bottles are stable with supports spanning from ~40 mm behind the base to the shoulder, i.e. a rack body depth of ~230–260 mm for 750 ml if the rear support is at the back wall; full-depth 343 racks simply put the rear rail at the back and the front rail at the front.

## 4. Packing math for a 335 × 335 mm opening

Assumptions:
- "Pitch" p = centre-to-centre spacing. Every bottle position is a circle of diameter p (bottle + half a wall each side) that must lie wholly inside the 335 square, i.e. centres in [p/2, 335 − p/2]. This counts bottles for an unlined opening where the outer frame already exists.
- Square grid: n = floor(335 / p) per row (see wall-thickness table below for the n·p − t ≤ 335 refinement).
- Hex/offset grid: row spacing p·√3/2; alternate rows hold n and n−1 unless (n + 1/2)·p ≤ 335.
- 45° lattice: the square grid rotated 45° inside the same window (bottle centres on a diamond lattice of pitch p), offset optimised numerically.
- Clear opening per cell = p − t for internal divider thickness t; bottles need ~4–6 mm clearance over body diameter for fingers/labels, so cell clear must be at least body dia + 5.
- Divider walls: for n cells across with n−1 internal walls of thickness t, n·p − t ≤ 335 (outer frame not counted). Candidate t: 0 (bare stacking / wire), 6 (sheet or thin ply), 12, 18 (board).

### 4.1 Bottle counts per arrangement

| Pitch p | Square grid | Hex/offset grid | 45° lattice | Clear cell (t=0 / 6 / 12 / 18) | Bottle types that fit the clear cell |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 85 | 3×3 = 9 (4×4 = 16 if t ≥ 5: 4·85 − t ≤ 335) | 4 rows (3,3,3,3) = 12 | 13 | 85 / 79 / 73 / 67 | t=0: Bordeaux 75–80, Alsace, cheap Burgundy. t≥6: Bordeaux ≤75 only |

Notes:
- Hex only beats square in the 85–90 pitch band (12 vs 9) because 4 rows fit vertically (p + 3·0.866p ≤ 335) while only 3 columns fit horizontally; at 80 the square's 4×4 wins; at ≥95 the two are equal or square wins. A hex pile is also what a loose bin of bottles naturally forms.
- The 45° lattice never beats the axis-aligned square grid in a square window; at p = 85 it ties the hex (13) but wastes corners. The only reason to rotate is aesthetics or the X-bin below.
- With 18 mm walls, the 335 opening gives 3 cells at 112 pitch with 94 clear (335 = 3×94 + 2×18 + 5 spare): this is the largest "everything standard fits" 3×3 option in board.
- With 12 mm walls: 3 cells at 112 pitch → 100 clear (335 = 3×100 + 2×12 + 11 spare), fits Krug/Comtes and Bordeaux magnums.
- With 6 mm (sheet) walls: 4 cells at 85 pitch → 79 clear (335 = 4×79 + 3×6 + 1 spare): a true 16-bottle Bordeaux-only grid.
- No divider (bottles stacked on each other): 4 Bordeaux (≤80) per row and 4 rows = 16 in square packing; Burgundy/Champagne (88–96) 3×3 = 9.

### 4.2 X-divider (four triangular bins) in the 335 square

Two diagonal boards of thickness t split the square into four right-isosceles triangles, hypotenuse 335 (the frame side), apex at the centre. Bottles pile loosely in each triangle (hex stacking, centres kept a half-diameter plus half a board from the diagonals):

| Bottle dia | t = 0 | t = 12 | t = 18 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 76 (Bordeaux) | 3 per bin (2+1) → 12 total | 3 per bin → 12 | 2 per bin → 8 |
| 80 | 3 → 12 | 2 → 8 | 2 → 8 |
| 85–90 (Burgundy) | 2 → 8 | 2 → 8 | 2 → 8 |
| 96 (Vintage Champagne) | 2 → 8 | 1 → 4 | 1 → 4 |
| 100 (Bordeaux magnum) | 1 → 4 | 1 → 4 | 1 → 4 |
| 115 (Champagne magnum) | 1 → 4 | 1 → 4 | 1 → 4 |

The X-bin is the worst-capacity option in a 335 square: 8–12 bottles versus 16 for a plain 4×4 grid; its merit is that bottles cannot roll out and any diameter fits.

### 4.3 The whole 335 opening used as a single diamond bin (opening rotated 45°, bottles pack square-lattice against the two lower walls)

| Bottle dia | Per side | Total |
|---|---|---|
| 76–80 (Bordeaux) | 4 | 16 |
| 85–100 (Burgundy, Champagne, Bordeaux magnum) | 3 | 9 |
| 115 (Champagne magnum) | 2 | 4 |

This matches commercial practice (12"–13" diamonds hold 12–16 Bordeaux [WN1]).

### 4.4 Recommendation by use

- Mixed cellar (Bordeaux + Burgundy + standard Champagne): 3×3 at 105–112 pitch with ≤12 mm walls (clear ≥ 93), or 3×3 bare stacking at 110 pitch.
- Bordeaux/Riesling-only density: 4×4 at 80–85 pitch, walls ≤6 mm (clear ≥ 79), or no walls.
- Prestige Champagne and Bordeaux magnums: 3×3 at 110–112 pitch, walls ≤12 mm (clear ≥ 98).
- Champagne/Burgundy magnums (115–117 dia): 2×2 at ≥125 pitch in a 335 square (clear ≥ 120).
