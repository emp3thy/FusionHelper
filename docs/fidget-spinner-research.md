### Judge comments per design

**1. Pentaroule 60 — Reuleaux Pentagon Constant-Width Spinner**
- play-value: The roll-between-fingers second mode and spin-to-circle reveal earn real repeat-use value, but the smooth dimple-only silhouette makes spin-up a chore, and it splits its novelty with Design 3.
- novelty: The constant-width roll mode and spin-to-circle illusion are genuinely unclaimed territory, but a smooth convex rim with only friction dimples is flick-starved, and its novelty is diluted by sharing the exact same Reuleaux claim as Design 3.
- print-engineer: A pure flat extrusion with vertical bore, 45-degree chamfers, and nut ceilings landing 0.3 mm over steel — the safest build in the field; only weaknesses are friction-limited dimple flicks and axial bearing retention by friction alone.

**2. Counterfeit Comet**
- play-value: The disbelief-then-glass-smooth reveal is a superb party trick in genuinely unclaimed territory, but with one flick lobe and a slow cadence the daily play value fades once the joke lands, and true balance depends on a fussy 100%-infill-plus-BB-trim workflow.
- novelty: Asymmetric-looking-but-balanced is verified unclaimed territory and the parametric CoM-nulling plus BB trim ring is what makes the trick reproducible rather than a one-off, though the payoff is more social prank than sustained fidget and the balance is hostage to 100% infill discipline.
- print-engineer: The geometry itself prints trivially — flat, support-free, nut-supported roofs — but the core trick depends on CAD-to-print mass fidelity that per-printer flow variance breaks, so the BB trim ring is load-bearing and the wow moment requires a manual balancing session.

**3. Vernier Contra-Ring**
- play-value: The clutch/differential feel is a tactile sensation no single-rotor spinner offers and the locked-mode flywheel is legit, but the 0.5 mm print-in-place diamond journal is fuse-or-gritty on uncalibrated machines.
- novelty: Coaxial counter-rotating rings with a dual-friction architecture (steel bearing under a printed journal) is a real new mechanism with three honest play modes, but the 0.5 mm print-in-place diamond joint is one over-squished first layer away from a fused paperweight.
- print-engineer: The coaxial counter-rotating architecture is genuinely new and the three play modes are real, but the whole concept hangs on a 0.5 mm print-in-place 45-degree diamond journal that will fuse on over-extruding machines and ridge on seam placement — a calibrated-printer design, not a farm-safe one.

**4. The Governor**
- play-value: State changes you can hear and feel — the gear-shift clack, stiffening gyro, and death rattle are exactly what makes people reach for a toy again — but stacked PIP slider gaps, 0.3 mm detents, and lip bridges over moving parts make first-print success a coin flip.
- novelty: A detent-gated centrifugal inertia shift you can hear and feel is a genuine state machine no published printable offers, but the design bets everything on 0.3 mm print-in-place clearances under bridged T-slot lips and 0.3 mm detents that wear.
- print-engineer: The best play concept in the field — an audible, tactile gear shift — but 0.3 mm vertical PIP clearances under 2.3 mm lip bridges, 0.3 mm detent ridges, and an integral rim that blocks the stated fallback make first-try success on a stock printer roughly a coin flip.

**5. Maelstrom Reticle**
- play-value: The moire spin-down speedometer is the most original visual in the field, but the reticle forces pinch-grip-only play, and a warp-prone 1.6 mm disc holding a 0.8 mm air gap plus BB pause makes reliable results calibration-heavy.
- novelty: Mounting the sampling reticle on the stationary cap so the zoetrope works in steady daylight is the single most original idea in the field — grounded in real optics, plus a Leblanc BB auto-balancer — but four parts, a warp-prone 1.6 mm disc, and a gap-sensitive 0.8 mm air clearance make it the fiddliest build.
- print-engineer: Brilliant optics, but 0.8/0.9 mm BB-channel walls at the slicing floor, a warp-prone 1.6 mm slitted reticle, pattern grooves on the first layer where elephant foot lives, and a rub-critical 0.8 mm air gap stack four calibration-sensitive failure modes on the core feature.

**6. Reuleaux Seven**
- play-value: Heavy penny-flywheel with seven flick landmarks and the 50p joke is charming, but 96 g at 80 mm fatigues the hand and the 1.7-1.9 mm inter-pocket webs sit right at the printable floor.
- novelty: The 50p cultural hook and coins-inside-a-coin joke are legible and the C7 execution is rigorous, but it is the second constant-width entry in one field, its smooth silhouette shares Design 0's weak flick torque, and 1.7-1.9 mm webs sit right at the printable floor.
- print-engineer: Same bulletproof flat-extrusion recipe as Design 0 with coin ceilings printed directly onto supported metal, docked a point for 1.68-1.94 mm webs that sit right at the perimeter-count floor and a 96 g heft that outruns the comfort band.

**7. Cicada**
- play-value: The pitch-glissando spin-down plus a deliberate ratchet-clicker mode covers two beloved fidget verbs in one object with a silent fallback, though 0.6 mm teeth, 0.8 mm flexures, and concentricity sensitivity will need iteration.
- novelty: Ratchet clickers exist, but tooth count as a pitch parameter feeding a Helmholtz rim soundbox elevates it from noisemaker to instrument with a swap-cap silent mode — at the cost of fatigue-prone 0.8 mm flexure pawls, sub-millimeter teeth, and drag that kills spin time.
- print-engineer: The click-not-whistle acoustics are correctly engineered and the swap-cap silent mode is smart, but 0.2 mm ratchet tooth tips at a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.8 mm flexure pawls, and volume that warbles with stub/socket concentricity mean the sound quality is printer-dependent by the spec's own admission.

**8. Maelstrom P5**
- play-value: A well-engineered, dead-easy print with good flick cadence and real rim mass, but spiral-look spinners exist on print sites and the play experience is ultimately a conventional armed spinner.
- novelty: Curved and swept-arm tri/penta spinners are far more common on print sites than the pitch admits — this is the field's closest thing to an arm-spinner reskin, well-executed and trivially printable but differentiated mostly by styling and an engraved illusion.
- print-engineer: Open-top nut pockets sidestep the bridging problem entirely and the fillet-guarded cusps are the only first-layer worry; a handsome, competent design whose spiral novelty is real but thinner than the mechanism-driven entries.

**9. Quoin**
- play-value: Beautifully printable machined-pebble discretion, but the gentle quad pulse and weak flick moment arm make it the design most likely to be admired once and left in a drawer.
- novelty: Hiding rim weights at a squircle's radius maxima is clever engineering, but a rounded-square coin with no visible mechanism reads as subtle to the point of anonymous on a thumbnail grid, and the snag-free virtue is also a flick liability.
- print-engineer: The most manufacturable design here — convex footprint, no thin webs, nut-supported ceilings, stepped bore with proper ledge — but the deliberately subtle squircle pulse and pebble aesthetic make it the least exciting to flick and the least visually distinct from a generic coin spinner.

**10. Gyre-70**
- play-value: The floating-lattice look with hidden steel is striking and the ring physics are sound, but play is a standard ring spinner, and the scripted cylindrical gyroid plus 1.2 mm lattice walls demand CAD and slicer skill most makers lack.
- novelty: The cylindrically-mapped gyroid solving lattice balance is a real engineering contribution and the floating-rim look is striking, but it requires a scripted mesh pipeline most makers won't run, 1.2 mm lattice walls that slice inconsistently, and a 0.85 mm pocket wall.
- print-engineer: The cylindrically-mapped gyroid is a genuinely clever balance solution and mostly self-supporting, but 1.2 mm lattice walls, a 0.85 mm pocket wall, and a scripted-mesh CAD dependency put it a tier below the flat extrusions for farm reliability.


## Judge Panel Summaries

- **play-value**: A strong field with several genuinely unclaimed mechanisms (variable inertia, counter-rotating moire, stator-mounted reticle, tuned ratchet acoustics) rather than the usual reskins. The clear pattern is an inverse correlation between novelty and print reliability: the designs with the highest play value (Governor, Vernier, Cicada) lean on print-in-place clearances, flexures, or sub-millimeter features, while the bulletproof flat extrusions (Quoin, Maelstrom P5) are the least differentiated. The two Reuleaux constant-width entries overlap heavily with each other, splitting what would otherwise be one standout niche.
- **novelty**: A strong field with real novelty in the mechanism-driven entries (Vernier Contra-Ring, Maelstrom Reticle, Governor, Counterfeit Comet) and honest but softer differentiation in the smooth-silhouette shapes. The two Reuleaux entries cannibalize each other's claimed niche, and the spiral-arm design is the closest thing to a reskinned arm spinner in disguise. Printability is inversely correlated with novelty here: the flat 2D-extrusion shapes are near-bulletproof, while the print-in-place joints, flexure pawls, and lattice bodies carry real failure modes.
- **print-engineer**: A strong field that splits cleanly into two camps: flat 2D-profile extrusions with pause-inserted metal (0, 3, 4, 5, 9) that will print first-try on any stock machine, and mechanism/optics designs (1, 2, 6, 8) whose core feature rides on print-in-place clearances, sub-millimeter flexures, or precision registration that will not survive every printer's calibration. The specs are unusually literate about FDM constraints — nearly every design cites correct seat interference, chamfers, and bridge limits — so the discriminator is how much the headline feature depends on fragile geometry.

## Topic: physics-theory

# Fidget Spinner Physics and Design Theory

## Moment of inertia and mass distribution
Spin behavior is governed by I = Σmr²: inertia grows with the square of the distance of mass from the axis, so rim-weighting is the highest-leverage design decision. High-quality spinners concentrate mass in the outer lobes as far from center as possible; a heavier, more spread-out spinner has more angular momentum (L = Iω) to lose to bearing friction and outlasts a compact one at the same initial speed (source: https://science-decor.com/blogs/science/how-does-a-fidget-spinner-work-the-physics-explained). Center-weighted designs feel "dead" — quick to start, quick to stop, weak gyroscopic push-back. The shape sets the moment of inertia and the bearing sets friction losses; together those two factors fully determine spin duration and smoothness (source: https://science-decor.com/...). Measured MOI experiments use physical pendulums or Tracker video analysis at 240 fps; Rhett Allain's pendulum-measured toy spinner spun ~104 s (sources: https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/pte/article/56/9/639/1016793, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325741350_Fidget_Spinner_Physics). Tungsten counterweight inserts (density ~19.3 g/cm³) at the lobe tips can extend spin time roughly 3x over standard spinners (source: https://www.etsy.com/listing/551342981/tungsten-fidget-spinner-weights-set-of-3).

## Spin decay
Angular velocity decays roughly exponentially, ω(t) = ω₀·e^(−t/τ); one observed spinner halved its speed every ~40 s. Because decay is exponential, doubling flick speed adds only about one time constant of extra spin (~20% in the observed case), so inertia and bearing quality beat flick strength (sources: https://interestingengineering.com/innovation/the-physics-of-fidget-spinners, https://science-decor.com/...).

## Bearings
- **608** (8×22×7 mm): the standardized skate/scooter bearing; cheap and ubiquitous, but its large 22 mm OD eats body volume and it needs more spinner mass to run efficiently — found on larger/budget spinners (source: https://www.abetterkit.com/best-fidget-spinner-bearings/).
- **R188** (1/4" bore × 1/2" OD × 3/16" wide; 6.35×12.7×4.76 mm): dominates premium spinners because the small footprint leaves more of the spinner's mass budget for the rim — a better mass-to-bearing ratio — and the smaller race radius has lower friction torque. Usually paired with press-fit grip buttons over the bearing (sources: https://www.abetterkit.com/best-fidget-spinner-bearings/, https://fidgetdoctor.com/best-fidget-spinner-bearings/).
- **Materials**: steel is cheapest and slowest (poor heat dissipation, possible rust). Hybrid ceramic (steel races + Si₃N₄ or ZrO₂ balls) is the enthusiast default — lighter balls, less rolling resistance, up to ~6x the spin of a basic bearing. Full ceramic gives maximum spin and is corrosion-proof and self-lubricating, but is fragile, noisier, and transfers more vibration (some users like that tactile feedback) (sources: https://www.bocabearings.com/blogs/2017/boca-bearings-guide-for-fidget-spinners, https://www.abetterkit.com/best-fidget-spinner-bearings/).
- **Shields**: open (unshielded) bearings are preferred — no shield/seal contact friction and easy cleaning; ZZ shielded protects from lint but ships greased. Spinner bearings are run dry: lubricant cuts spin time 30–70%; clean with isopropyl alcohol (sources: https://www.abetterkit.com/..., https://www.mightyedc.com/blogs/news-unboxing/how-to-clean-and-maintain-your-fidget-spinner-like-a-pro). Dry, clean bearings give 2–5 min spins on standard spinners and 8–12+ min on high-precision ones (source: https://www.mightyedc.com/...). ABEC-7/9 ratings appear in marketing but tolerance class matters less than dryness, ball material, and openness (source: https://science-decor.com/...).

## Balance and imbalance
Any center-of-mass offset from the bearing axis produces a rotating centripetal force → felt vibration, audible buzz, wobble, faster spin-down, and reduced bearing life. Premium CNC production holds bearing-seat concentricity to the outer profile within ~5 μm, machines material removal evenly across arms, and spin-balances parts to find where to add/remove weight (sources: https://topsbest-precision.com/industry/cnc-machined-fidget-spinner/, https://motorcityrepair.com/measuring-cnc-spindle-vibration-balance-a-repair-guide/). Balanced arms minimize wobble and energy loss from uneven per-arm inertia (source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Fidget_spinner).

## Size and weight for hand feel
Typical spinner: ~52 mm OD, ~68 g, ~21 mm arm length — this size pockets well for EDC; 60–65 mm models snag on fabric. Practical range: 50–90 mm diameter, ~60–70 g standard; heavier builds (brass/tungsten, 100 g+) trade portability for spin time and stronger gyro feel (sources: https://www.aliexpress.com/s/wiki-ssr/article/dimensions-of-a-fidget-spinner, https://fidget-spinner.net/blog/1733736685).

## Gyroscopic effect
A spinning spinner is a pocket gyroscope: tilting it fights the angular momentum vector, and the applied torque emerges 90° later in the rotation direction (precession), so the device tilts diagonally and pushes back with "no obvious source" — the core of the fidget sensation. The effect scales with L = Iω, so rim-weighted, fast spinners feel most alive (sources: https://interestingengineering.com/innovation/the-physics-of-fidget-spinners, https://physicsforums.com/threads/exploring-the-gyroscopic-forces-of-a-figgit-spinner.918013).

## Lobes and flick mechanics
Two- and three-lobed variants are the standard forms (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidget_spinner). Three symmetric lobes give a lobe under the finger every 120° for easy repeated flicking and easy catching/stopping; a two-lobe bar concentrates its mass budget into two far tips (high I per gram) but presents a lobe only every 180° and pulses more in the hand. Flick spin-up obeys τ = Iα: higher inertia means slower acceleration per flick — heavy rim-weighted spinners need several flicks or a hard snap to reach speed (source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Fidget_spinner). Finger torque = force × moment arm, so longer arms and grippable outer-edge geometry (notches, chamfers) make spin-up easier; the same protruding lobes that ease flicking also ease braking with a fingertip. Exponential decay makes flicking harder past a point pointless — design for inertia and low friction instead (source: https://science-decor.com/...). The premium segment began with Scott McCoskery's CNC-machined metal Torqbar (~2014), a two-lobe R188-button design (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidget_spinner).

**Key facts:**
- Moment of inertia scales with mass times radius squared (I = Σmr²), so rim-weighting the lobes is the single highest-leverage design choice for spin time and gyroscopic feel.
- Spin decays roughly exponentially (ω(t) = ω₀e^(−t/τ)); doubling flick speed adds only about one time constant of spin time, so inertia and bearing quality matter more than flick strength.
- R188 bearings measure 6.35 x 12.7 x 4.76 mm (1/4 x 1/2 x 3/16 in) versus 22 mm OD for a 608; the small footprint leaves more mass budget for the rim, which is why R188 dominates premium spinners.
- 608 bearings are the cheap standardized skate bearing and need larger, heavier spinner bodies to perform; they define the budget tier.
- Hybrid ceramic bearings (steel races, Si3N4 or ZrO2 balls) are the enthusiast default, giving up to ~6x the spin time of a basic steel bearing; full ceramic spins longest but is fragile, noisier, and transmits more vibration.
- Open (unshielded) bearings run dry are standard for spinners: shields add contact friction, and lubricant cuts spin time 30-70%; cleaning is an isopropyl alcohol soak.
- Clean dry bearings yield 2-5 minute spins on standard spinners and 8-12+ minutes on high-precision builds; a typical toy spinner measured ~104 s.
- Any center-of-mass offset from the bearing axis causes vibration, buzz, wobble, and shortened spin; premium CNC production holds bearing-seat concentricity within ~5 microns and spin-balances parts.
- Typical hand-feel sweet spot: ~50-60 mm diameter and 60-70 g total (a common reference spinner is 52 mm / 68 g with 21 mm arms); 50-90 mm and up to ~100 g covers the practical range, with heavier builds trading pocketability for spin time.
- The fidget sensation is gyroscopic precession: tilting the spinner produces a resisting torque that emerges 90 degrees later in the rotation, scaling with L = Iω, so faster/heavier-rimmed spinners feel more alive.
- Flick spin-up follows τ = Iα: high-inertia spinners accelerate slowly per flick and need multiple flicks; longer arms and grippy outer-edge geometry increase the finger's moment arm and ease acceleration.
- Three lobes present a flickable surface every 120 degrees (easy repeated flicks and catches); two-lobe bars pack more inertia per gram at the tips but present lobes only every 180 degrees and pulse more in the hand.
- Tungsten (density ~19.3 g/cm3) counterweights at lobe tips can extend spin time roughly 3x over a standard spinner of the same size.
- The premium spinner category started with Scott McCoskery's CNC-machined Torqbar (~2014), a two-lobe design using an R188 bearing with press-fit grip buttons.

## Topic: proven-shapes

# Fidget Spinner Shape Research

## Core physics (applies to every archetype)
Spin quality = moment of inertia (shape/mass distribution) x bearing friction losses. Mass pushed to the perimeter raises I, so the same flick energy yields longer, smoother spins; rotation decays roughly exponentially (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidget_spinner, https://science-decor.com/blogs/science/how-does-a-fidget-spinner-work-the-physics-explained). Protrusions (arms) put graspable mass off-axis, which is what makes flick-starting possible; one review found a quad flicks more easily than a tri (source: https://www.livescience.com/58963-how-fidget-spinners-work-physics.html via search snippet). A cheap commercial spinner spins ~104 s in lab measurement (source: https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/pte/article/56/9/639/1016793). The Cambridge record "Pulsar" formalized the design problem: maximize initial rotational energy, minimize terminal energy, and slow the transition — three conflicting goals with no closed-form optimum. Its answer: hollow aluminum core, tungsten distributed around the rim; 30 min 34.54 s Guinness record, ~20x a store spinner (source: https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/cambridge-fidget-spinner-guinness-record). MinebeaMitsumi's earlier 24:46 record spinner (50 engineers, 6 months) was likewise a ring: heavy outside, light middle, thin high-strength aluminum/brass wheels around premium ball bearings (source: https://gizmodo.com/50-engineers-spent-six-months-designing-a-fidget-spinne-1823694959, https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/commercial/2018/2/video-man-spins-fidget-spinner-on-his-finger-for-24-minutes-513516).

## Bearings (shape-independent but decisive)
608 skate bearing (8x22x7 mm) is the mass-market standard (source: https://www.mklbearing.com/blog/what-size-are-fidget-spinner-bearings-_b43). Premium spinners use the smaller R188 (6.35x12.7x4.76 mm): at equal spinner mass an R188 build outspins a 608 build (smaller race radius = lower friction torque), and hybrid-ceramic (Si3N4/ZrO2 balls) versions spin longer and quieter (source: https://fidgetdoctor.com/best-fidget-spinner-bearings/, https://fidgetgear.com/products/fidget-spinner-buttons-and-adapter-608-to-r188-bearing-stainless-steel).

## Archetypes
**Two-arm bar** ("peanut," the Torqbar archetype). Scott McCoskery (MD Engineering, Suquamish WA) created the Torqbar ~2014; USPTO recognizes him as sole inventor of the modern bearing spinner (provisional patent May 2016, utility patent Mar 2018). Ergonomics dictated the peanut shape — length, width, thickness sized for "maximum fidgetability" (source: https://torqbar.com/pages/press-release, https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/our-high-end-fidget-spinner-will-beat-the-competition-local-makers-say/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidget_spinner). Bars are the EDC favorite: compact, flat, pocketable. Weakness: only two arms, so any machining asymmetry shows up as wobble — this is why bar spinners are dominated by precision CNC makers. Torqbar weights by material: CP2 titanium 31.1 g, 303 stainless 54.1 g, copper ~60 g, tungsten-copper ~99 g, Magnum SS 114 g; $200+ price class (source: https://torqbar.com/products/torqbar-magnum-stainless-steel-blasted-tumbled-with-magnum-big-dish-buttons, https://www.forbes.com/sites/jplafke/2016/12/23/the-199-torqbar-is-the-iphone-of-desk-toys/).

**Tri-arm.** The mass-market default. Three evenly balanced arms give predictable rotation, multiple tactile grip landmarks, easy thumb-flicks, and tolerance to imperfect balance; mass at three arm tips gives good I (source: https://www.memorytech.com.au/blogs/memory-tech-insights-9/62d3d17d-6368-4eac-a5c3-09a0feef498d-metal-fidget-spinner-comparison-memory-tech). Typical plastic tris: ~7 cm across, 60-120 g (2-4 oz), 608 bearing, 1.5-3 min spins (source: https://www.abetterkit.com/best-fidget-spinner/).

**Quad-arm.** Higher mass and I; easier to flick-start per reviewer observation. Costs: heavier, bulkier, worse pocket carry; heavier spinners (>100 g) fatigue the hand and carry poorly. Guides put the optimal all-round weight at 60-90 g (source: https://smartbuy.alibaba.com/buyingguides/fidget-spinner, https://fidget-spinner.net/blog/1732603150).

**Ring/wheel.** Physically optimal: a rim concentrates all mass at max radius (I = mr² vs ½mr² for a solid disc), which is why both world-record spinners are rings. Trade-offs: fewer grip protrusions, larger diameter for the same feel, less "arm" feedback. Some ring products skip the bearing entirely (manual flick jewelry rings) (source: https://wellwhisk.com/best-fidget-rings/).

**Coin/disc.** Most discreet and pocketable; lower I per gram than a ring, so spin times lag unless rim-weighted. Survives mostly as hybrid coin/spinner collectibles (e.g., discontinued Energy Tower) (source: https://www.mightyedc.com/blogs/news-unboxing/beginner-s-guide-to-7-types-of-fidget-spinners).

**Crossbar/propeller.** Bar variants with perpendicular or blade-like arms; mostly aesthetic — thin blades lower I, and these remained niche 3D-print/novelty categories (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2294202).

**Aerodisc.** Enclosed-rim disc style; almost no independent documentation survives — treat as a rim-weighted disc variant. (Search results conflate it with Rotiform car wheels.)

- **ZeroFeud Compass line** — Ti/SS/brass bar spinners; full-size Ti Compass "sells out in under 10 seconds"; Compass Micro: 1.45 x 0.52 x 0.39 in, hybrid-ceramic R188, $125; community shootouts say it outspins Torqbar (source: https://zerofeud.myshopify.com/products/zerofeud-compass-micro, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1AQEooI0TZU).

## Shared geometric traits of the best commercial spinners
1) Mass at the perimeter, light hub (dense button/rim inserts: brass, copper, tungsten). 2) Compact bar footprint ~40-55 mm long, 30-45 g Ti / 50-60 g steel — pocketable, 60-90 g ceiling. 3) Removable R188 hybrid-ceramic bearing with screw-apart serviceable buttons. 4) Concave/dished ~20 mm grip buttons with rounded edges so fingers don't slip or pinch; bead-blasted/tumbled matte finish for grip (source: https://www.mightyedc.com/blogs/news-unboxing/the-ultimate-guide-to-fidget-spinners-2025-edition). 5) Strict rotational symmetry — balance beats raw mass; wobble kills perceived quality.

**Key facts:**
- Spin performance is governed by moment of inertia (mass pushed to the perimeter) versus bearing friction; rotation decays roughly exponentially.
- Both world-record spinners were ring/wheel designs: MinebeaMitsumi's heavy-rim/light-middle ring spun 24:46 and Cambridge's Pulsar (hollow aluminum core, tungsten rim) spun 30:34.54 — about 20x a store-bought spinner.
- A typical cheap commercial tri-spinner spins about 104 seconds in laboratory measurement (AIP Physics Teacher).
- The 608 bearing (8x22x7 mm) is the mass-market standard; premium spinners use the smaller R188 (6.35x12.7x4.76 mm), which outspins a 608 at equal spinner mass, ideally in hybrid-ceramic form.
- Scott McCoskery's Torqbar (~2014, MD Engineering) is the original two-arm bar spinner; he holds the utility patent (filed May 2016, granted March 2018) and is USPTO-recognized as the inventor.
- Torqbar weights by material: CP2 titanium 31.1 g, 303 stainless 54.1 g, copper ~60 g, tungsten-copper ~99 g, Magnum 114 g; price class $199-$240.
- Rotablade Stubby (benchmark titanium bar): 50 x 30 x 9 mm body, 11.7 mm at grips, 40 g, ceramic hybrid bearing, consistent 3+ minute spins.
- ZeroFeud Compass Micro measures 1.45 x 0.52 x 0.39 in with a hybrid-ceramic R188 bearing at $125; full-size Ti Compasses sell out in under 10 seconds and outspin the Torqbar in community shootouts.
- Tri-arm is the dominant consumer shape because three balanced arms give predictable rotation, wobble tolerance, and multiple grip/flick landmarks; typical plastic tris are ~7 cm and spin 1.5-3 minutes.
- Quad-arm spinners flick-start more easily and carry more inertia but are heavier and less pocketable; buying guides put the optimal spinner weight at 60-90 g with a ~100 g portability ceiling.
- A rim (ring) has double the moment of inertia per gram of a solid disc at the same radius (mr^2 vs 0.5mr^2), which is why record designs are rings and why coin/disc styles trade spin time for discretion.
- Bar spinners are the EDC/premium community favorite for pocketability but demand precision machining because two arms amplify any imbalance into wobble.
- Best-in-class commercial spinners share: perimeter-concentrated mass with a light hub, ~40-55 mm bar footprint at 30-60 g, removable serviceable R188 hybrid-ceramic bearing, concave ~20 mm grip buttons with rounded edges, and bead-blasted matte finishes.
- Crossbar and propeller styles remain niche/novelty: thin blades reduce moment of inertia, and no premium maker built a reputation on them.
- High-end maker ecosystem: Torqbar/MD Engineering (WA), ZeroFeud, Rotablade (UK), BilletSPIN (WI, exotic alloys, hand-finished limited runs).

# FDM Printability Research: Small Handheld Parts (Fidget Spinners)

## Flat bottoms vs curved undersides
- A flat face on the build plate is the single most reliable geometry: no supports, maximum first-layer contact, best strength. Curved or domed undersides force supports, which leave scars on the contact surface and print poorly; consensus is to reorient so the cosmetic/mating surface points up or vertical rather than tuning supports (source: https://www.goodprints3d.com/blogs/3d/why-do-3d-print-support-scars-happen-and-what-should-you-change-first, https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=24438). Gentle curves on top/bottom faces show pronounced stair-stepping; vertical faces get the smoothest finish from XY resolution (source: https://www.art-designing.com/how-to-orient-3d-models-to-prevent-print-failures-and-weak-spots/). Conclusion: prism-like extrusions of a 2D profile (flat top and bottom) are the ideal spinner form; chamfer rather than fillet bottom edges (a bottom fillet is an unsupported curve; a 45° chamfer prints cleanly).
- Elephant's foot widens the bottom ~0.5 mm of the part by 0.1–0.3 mm and jams any fit at the base; a 0.5 mm x 45° chamfer on every bottom mating edge, plus slicer elephant-foot compensation, eliminates the problem (source: https://x3dstudios.com/blog/3d-printing-tolerances-guide, https://help.prusa3d.com — PrusaSlicer compensation per https://www.tomshardware.com/3d-printing/how-to-fix-elephant-foot-issues-in-3d-prints).

## Overhangs and bridges
- 45° from vertical is the safe overhang limit: each layer is ~50% supported by the one below. Well-cooled machines manage 55–60°, but designing to 45° works everywhere (source: https://www.aleader-china.com/blog/45-degree-rule-3d-printing-design-guide/, https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-parts-fdm-3d-printing/).
- Bridges (spans anchored both ends): 5–10 mm is reliably clean; quality degrades with length. Upper limits ~50 mm PLA, ~30 mm ABS/nylon with sag (source: https://layerx3d.in/blog/fdm-design-rules-wall-thickness-overhangs-bridging-tolerances, https://zbotic.in/3d-printing-overhangs-without-supports-bridging-tricks-that-actually-work/). For a bearing pocket that must be capped, keep any bridged ceiling ≤10 mm or chamfer/teardrop it.

## Bearing press-fit (608: 22 mm OD x 8 mm ID x 7 mm)
- Design interference of 0.05–0.10 mm per side (0.1–0.2 mm on diameter): PLA at the low end (~0.05 mm/side) because it is brittle and cracks under aggressive interference; PETG flexes and tolerates ~0.10 mm/side (source: https://tools.creative3dp.com/blog/press-fit-tolerances-3d-printing/, https://zbotic.in/3d-printing-tolerances-designing-gaps-for-press-fits-threads-and-snap-fits/). Published research on printed bearing housings models the seat 0.2 mm under nominal (source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/384892832_DETERMINING_3D_PRINTED_HOUSING_DIAMETERS_FOR_PRESS-FITTING_STANDARD_BALL_BEARINGS). Note holes also print undersized, so slicer/printer calibration shifts the real fit — test rings are standard practice (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/994179-608-bearing-high-tolerance-print-assemble).
- Anti-crack seat design: ≥3 perimeters around the bore, high infill (40%+ to 100%) near the seat, and a 0.5 mm x 45° lead-in chamfer; press with a vise not a hammer — "the chamfer does more for success rate than another 0.05 mm of anything" (source: https://tools.creative3dp.com/blog/press-fit-tolerances-3d-printing/). Popular spinner models use ~0.1 mm clearance/tight fits and 100% infill at ~0.16 mm layers (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/1831794-revised-fidget-spinner-608-bearing).

## Walls, weight, balance
- Minimum walls (0.4 mm nozzle): 0.8 mm absolute floor (2 perimeters); 1.2 mm+ recommended vertical; 1.5–2.0 mm for load-bearing/handled parts. Design wall thicknesses in multiples of 0.4 mm (0.8/1.2/1.6/2.0) for clean slicing (source: https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/dfm-tips-for-3d-printed-parts-with-thin-walls/, https://www.raise3d.com/blog/3d-printing-wall-thickness/, https://www.simplemachining.com/blogs/dfm-for-3d-printing-a-practical-design-guide).
- Perimeters beat infill for strength: two extra walls add more strength than +10% infill; 3–4 walls plus 30–50% infill is the fidget-toy sweet spot (source: https://www.snapmaker.com/blog/3d-printer-wall-thickness/, https://siraya.tech/blogs/news/3d-print-fidget-toys). For spin: mass at the rim increases moment of inertia — designs put bearings, nuts, or coins at the perimeter; symmetric lobes (3-fold typical) keep balance. Infill is deterministic per slicer, so identical lobes stay balanced; asymmetric geometry with sparse infill risks imbalance — prefer 100% infill or high wall count on small spinners (source: https://3dstartpoint.com/3d-printed-fidget-spinners/, https://www.printables.com/model/253500-penny-weight-fidget-spinners).

## Small-part bed adhesion
- Small footprints are knock-off-prone: use a brim (first defense), clean bed (IPA), correct first-layer height ~0.2 mm, first-layer speed 30–50% of normal, first-layer line width 100–110% of nozzle (source: https://www.sovol3d.com/blogs/news/why-small-parts-fail-in-3d-printing-and-easy-fixes, https://kingroon.com/blogs/3d-print-101/how-to-fix-3d-print-elephant-foot-with-initial-layer-1). Sharp plan-view corners concentrate shrinkage and curl; add corner radii (≥4 mm cited for large parts — smaller radii still help small parts) (source: https://thevirtualfoundry.com/3d-printing-corners-curling/).

## Features that reliably fail
- Anything thinner than the nozzle or below layer height; tall thin spires/pins wobble and get insufficient cooling time per layer (source: https://x3dstudios.com/blog/3d-printing-tolerances-guide, https://forum.prusa3d.com/forum/original-prusa-i3-mk3s-mk3-how-do-i-print-this-printing-help/tall-object-with-small-footprint/). Large flat overhangs/unsupported ceilings droop. Sharp internal corners are stress risers that start cracks (especially brittle PLA around press fits) — fillet internal corners. Sub-0.8 mm walls slice inconsistently.

## Vase mode & print-in-place
- Vase mode: one continuous single-perimeter spiral — no seam, fast, but one object per plate, no islands/bridges/top closures, structurally weak with 0.4 mm nozzle; unsuitable for spinners needing bearing seats and mass (source: https://www.obico.io/blog/orca-slicers-spiral-vase-vase-mode-a-deep-dive/, https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/software/bambu-studio/spiral-vase).
- Print-in-place clearances: 0.2–0.3 mm XY works on most calibrated printers (0.15 mm possible); hinges start at 0.3 mm per side; PETG fuses at clearances PLA tolerates — PLA ~0.3 mm sliding / 0.5 mm rotating, PETG ~0.4 / 0.6 mm; orient pins vertically for round profiles (source: https://blog.uavmodel.com/3d-printer-print-in-place-mechanism-design-clearance-tolerances-hinge-geometry-and-joint-guidelines-2026/, https://www.3dprinterstuff.com/workshop/design-print-in-place-hinges-that-work). Fully printed 608-replacement bearings exist but spin far worse than steel (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2316425).

## Captive weights
- Standard patterns: hex-nut pockets (M8/M10 nuts, hex pocket ~0.2-0.4 mm over nut across-flats) or coin slots (e.g., pennies) in the lobes; either open-top pockets or pause-at-height G-code to seal weights inside; caps bridge over the pocket so keep pocket spans small (source: https://www.instructables.com/3D-Printed-Hex-Nut-Fidget-Spinners/, https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2254972, https://www.printables.com/model/253500-penny-weight-fidget-spinners, https://www.instructables.com/Make-a-Seamless-Captive-Nut-in-a-3D-Printed-Part/).

**Key facts:**
- A flat bottom face on the build plate is the most reliable geometry; curved undersides need supports that scar the surface, so spinner bodies should be flat-bottomed 2D-profile extrusions.
- Overhangs up to 45 degrees from vertical print cleanly without supports; beyond that quality degrades rapidly (55-60 degrees only on well-cooled machines).
- Bridges print cleanly at 5-10 mm spans; PLA can technically bridge up to ~50 mm with sag, so bearing-pocket ceilings should stay at or under ~10 mm or be chamfered.
- A 608 bearing is 22 mm OD x 8 mm ID x 7 mm wide; design the printed seat 0.1-0.2 mm under 22 mm diameter (0.05 mm/side interference for PLA, up to 0.10 mm/side for PETG).
- PLA is brittle and cracks if press-fit interference is too aggressive; use at least 3 perimeters and 40-100% infill around the bore, plus a 0.5 mm x 45 degree lead-in chamfer.
- Minimum wall thickness with a 0.4 mm nozzle is 0.8 mm absolute, 1.2 mm recommended, 1.5-2.0 mm for handled/load-bearing parts; design walls in multiples of 0.4 mm.
- Extra perimeters add more strength than extra infill: two additional walls beat raising infill from 20% to 30%; 3-4 walls with 30-50%+ infill is the fidget-toy norm, 100% infill common for small spinners.
- Elephant's foot bulges the bottom ~0.5 mm of a print outward by 0.1-0.3 mm; put a 0.5 mm x 45 degree chamfer on all bottom edges and mating features.
- Small parts need a brim for bed adhesion, ~0.2 mm first layer, and first-layer speed at 30-50% of normal; sharp plan-view corners curl, so radius them.
- Reliably failing features: walls/pins thinner than the nozzle (under ~0.8 mm walls, ~2 mm pins), tall thin spires (wobble plus insufficient layer cooling), large flat unsupported overhangs, and sharp internal corners which act as crack starters.
- Print-in-place clearance sweet spot is 0.2-0.3 mm in XY for PLA (0.15 mm on well-calibrated machines); PETG fuses at PLA clearances and needs ~0.1 mm more (0.4 mm sliding, 0.6 mm rotating).
- Vase mode gives a seamless single-perimeter shell but forbids islands, bridges, infill, and top closures and is structurally weak, so it is unsuitable for bearing-seat spinners.
- Spinner spin time comes from moment of inertia: put mass at the rim via perimeter bearings, hex nuts (e.g. M8), or coins in symmetric (typically 3-fold) lobes to keep balance.
- Captive nuts/coins are embedded either in open pockets or sealed via a G-code pause-at-height, with the covering layers bridging the pocket, so pocket spans must stay small.
- Printed hole diameters come out undersized versus CAD and vary with calibration, so a printed test ring for the bearing seat is standard practice before committing to a full spinner print.

# Fidget spinner geometry research

## 1. What the market looks like (baseline)

Surveys of Printables/Thingiverse/Cults/MakerWorld listings confirm the tri-arm clone hypothesis. A 110-design roundup on 3DPrinterChat shows tri-arm spinners are ~half of all designs, with the remainder mostly character/IP-themed reskins, star/angular variants, and a handful of 4+ arm versions — variation is achieved "through surface texture, weight distribution, and thematic ornamentation rather than structural innovation" (source: https://medium.com/3dprinterchat/110-fidget-spinner-designs-to-3d-print-part-i-ce062c4fceae). Thingiverse's fidget-spinner tag is dominated by 608-skate-bearing tri-spinners from 2016-2017 (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/tag:fidget_spinner). The de facto hardware standard is the 608 skateboard bearing: 22 mm OD, 8 mm bore, 7 mm wide (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2075716). The second major cluster is print-in-place bearingless spinners — muzz64's one-piece spinner with four printed-in bearings (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2237509) and newer "X-bearing" print-in-place geometry designs (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/1135300-fidget-spinner-print-in-place-no-hardware). The third cluster, now very popular on MakerWorld, is print-in-place planetary-gear spinners — e.g. six 12-tooth planets around a 24-tooth sun in a 55 mm body, 15 g filament, 1.5 h print (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/1932158-planetary-gears-finger-fidget-spinner), and a herringbone-gear "Planetary Vortex" that spins well vertical or horizontal (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/2134377-planetary-vortex-fidget-spinner). So planetary gears and print-in-place are common; treat them as table stakes, not differentiators.

## 2. Printable-but-underused geometry classes (evidence per class)

- **Gyroid/TPMS**: only trivial examples exist — "Basic Gyroid Fidget Spinner Keychains" (source: https://www.myminifactory.com/object/3d-print-basic-gyroid-fidget-spinner-keychains-352350). TPMS research confirms gyroid prints reliably in FDM PLA with good compressive strength (source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11053662/). A gyroid-shell spinner body with rim mass is essentially unexplored.
- **Reuleaux polygons**: used as rock-in-frame fidgets (triangle in square frame, 0.25 mm PIP clearance) but NOT as spinner rotor profiles (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/2990886-reuleaux-triangle-fidget). Constant-width rotor as spinner silhouette is open territory.
- **Superellipse/squircle**: exists as a stacked sliding fidget ("Superellipse Fidget", MakerWorld) but not as a spinner profile (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/538174-superellipse-fidget).
- **Fibonacci/spiral/involute**: one 2017 Fibonacci-spiral tri-spinner exists (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2075716); logarithmic-spiral arms with balanced mass remain rare.
- **Nested gimbal rings**: an emerging niche — 3-ring print-in-place gyroscope spinner (source: https://www.printables.com/model/1619262-gyroscope-fidget-spinner-print-in-place-3-nested-g), 7-ring gyro spinner (source: https://makerworld.com/en/models/624400-the-3x-more-amazing-7-ring-gyro-spinner-fidget), gyroscopic fidget rings with leveling effects (source: https://www.instructables.com/Gyroscopic-Fidget-Rings/). Present but far from saturated; combining gimbal rings WITH a bearing spinner is rarer.
- **Chainmail/articulated**: chainmail prints-in-place reliably and is a popular fidget class (source: https://www.printables.com/model/1270127-chainmail-fidget) but is never combined with a spinner — a rotor with chainmail-draped arms or articulated flails is unexplored.
- **Odd rotational symmetry (5/7-fold)**: market is 2/3/6-arm; a "Quintuple Spinner" and "PentaFlex" exist but odd-count symmetry is genuinely rare (sources: https://medium.com/3dprinterchat/110-fidget-spinner-designs-to-3d-print-part-i-ce062c4fceae, https://makerworld.com/en/search/models?keyword=fidget+spinner).
- **Asymmetric-yet-balanced (Halbach-like)**: no hits found for spinners; only commercial "weird asymmetric" novelty spinners (source: https://www.aliexpress.com/s/wiki-ssr/article/weirdest-fidget-spinners). Any mass layout with CoM on axis spins fine — a visually lopsided but statically balanced rotor is an open, cheap differentiator.

## 3. What makes a spinner read as 'markedly different'

- **Animation while spinning**: zoetrope fidget spinners (Jonathan Odom's cat-run zoetrope; slit drum + sequential frames) are proven and viral (source: https://www.3ders.org/articles/20170617-get-your-daily-cat-animation-fill-with-this-3d-printed-zoetrope-fidget-spinner.html); phenakistoscope variants exist (Instructables darlingtom). Stroboscopic versions animate under flickering LED/fluorescent light with no electronics — Printables "Strobe Spinning Top" (source: https://www.printables.com/model/193799-strobe-spinning-top); John Edmark's Blooms use golden-angle (137.5°) frame steps (source: https://www.thisiscolossal.com/2017/01/blooms-2-strobe-animated-sculptures-by-john-edmark/).
- **Moire effects**: counter-rotating spoked discs on a common axis produce moving moire interference (source: https://hackaday.com/2020/01/04/3d-printed-moire-illusion-may-just-drive-you-crazy/); a two-rotor spinner where finger-spin counter-rotates two printed grids would be novel. Spiral discs give expand/contract illusion + motion aftereffect, a documented perceptual effect (source: https://cen.acs.org/articles/95/i32/Spirals-spinners-science.html); hypnotic spiral spinner STL exists (source: https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:2321394).
- **Sound**: whistling tops work (edge sharpness inside whistle opening controls tone; smaller nozzle = sharper edge = louder) (source: https://www.printables.com/model/1776071-whistlespin-whistling-spinning-top); a whistling fidget SPINNER attempt with reed chambers at bearing housings failed to sound (source: https://cults3d.com/en/3d-model/game/fidget-non-whistling-spinner) — air-driven sound on hand-spun rims is hard at low RPM; clicker/clacker rhythmic sound per rotation is the reliable route.
- **Variable geometry**: patented "angular momentum spinner" uses sliding blocks on arms to change moment of inertia mid-spin (figure-skater effect) (source: https://patents.justia.com/ US10525366); transforming spinners with flip/reconfigure arms exist commercially (source: https://www.instructables.com/FidgSpin-the-Transforming-Fidget-Spinner/). Almost absent from print-file platforms — a PIP sliding-mass spinner is a strong differentiator.
- **Physics constraints**: spin time is set by moment of inertia (mass at rim) vs bearing friction; quality designs concentrate mass in outer lobes (source: https://science-decor.com/blogs/science/how-does-a-fidget-spinner-work-the-physics-explained). Typical good spin ~104 s (source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325741350_Fidget_Spinner_Physics). Any novel silhouette must still put printed or coin/bearing mass at max radius and keep CoM on the bearing axis.

**Key facts:**
- Tri-arm spinners are roughly half of all listed designs; most others are reskins (character/star themes), so any non-tri-arm silhouette already reads as different (3DPrinterChat 110-design survey).
- The de facto hardware standard is the 608 skateboard bearing: 22 mm OD, 8 mm bore, 7 mm wide.
- Print-in-place bearingless spinners and print-in-place planetary-gear spinners are now common on MakerWorld (e.g. 6x12-tooth planets around a 24-tooth sun, 55 mm, 15 g, 1.5 h print) - they are table stakes, not differentiators.
- Gyroid/TPMS geometry prints reliably in FDM PLA but appears only in trivial keychain spinners - a gyroid-shell rotor is essentially unexplored.
- Reuleaux triangles are used as rock-in-frame fidgets (0.25 mm print-in-place clearance) but never as spinner rotor profiles.
- Superellipse/squircle exists as a stacked sliding fidget but not as a spinner silhouette.
- Odd rotational symmetry (5-fold, 7-fold) is genuinely rare; the market is 2/3/6-arm.
- Any mass layout with center of mass on the bearing axis spins fine, so visually asymmetric-but-balanced (Halbach-like) rotors are physically easy and unclaimed.
- Zoetrope/phenakistoscope spinners (slits + sequential frames) are proven and viral; stroboscopic patterns animate under flickering LED light with zero electronics (Edmark Blooms use 137.5-degree golden-angle steps).
- Counter-rotating printed grids on one axis create moving moire patterns; spiral discs give a documented expand/contract motion-aftereffect illusion.
- Air-driven whistling works on high-RPM tops (edge sharpness sets tone) but a reed-chamber whistling fidget spinner failed to sound at hand-spin RPM; per-rotation clicker sound is the reliable acoustic route.
- Variable-geometry spinners with sliding masses (figure-skater moment-of-inertia change) are patented (US10525366) but almost absent from print-file platforms.
- Nested gimbal-ring gyro spinners (3-7 print-in-place rings) are an emerging but unsaturated niche.
- Spin time is governed by moment of inertia vs bearing friction: mass must go at maximum radius; a good spinner runs about 104 seconds.
- Chainmail and articulated structures print-in-place reliably and are popular fidgets, but no design combines them with a spinning rotor.

# Design Specifications (all 10)


## Design 0: Pentaroule 60 — Reuleaux Pentagon Constant-Width Spinner

**Direction:** Reuleaux polygon family: a spinner whose silhouette is a Reuleaux polygon (constant-width shape). Exploit the odd-symmetry balance.

**Concept:** A fidget spinner whose entire silhouette is a rounded Reuleaux pentagon — a true constant-width curve, 60 mm wide in every direction. It has no arms at all: the five steel-nut masses hide inside the solid constant-width body, giving 5-fold odd symmetry that is genuinely rare in the market. Because the shape is constant-width it doubles as a second fidget: it rolls smoothly between two fingers or under a flat object with zero bobbing, like an oversized 50p coin, and when spun it visually "becomes" a perfect circle, snapping back to a pentagon as it stops.

**Geometry:** Overall: constant width W = 60.0 mm in all directions (fits 55-90 mm requirement), prism thickness T = 9.0 mm, flat top and bottom faces. SILHOUETTE (rounded Reuleaux pentagon, exact construction): user parameters W = 60, n = 5, corner radius rho = 3.0. Pentagon diagonal d = W - 2*rho = 54.0 mm; place 5 vertex points on a construction circle of radius R_v = d / (2*sin(72 deg)) = 28.39 mm, spaced 72 deg. Profile = 5 side arcs of radius (W - rho) = 57.0 mm, each centered on a vertex and spanning between the two opposite vertices' corner circles, joined tangentially by 5 corner arcs of radius rho = 3.0 mm centered on the vertices. Tip radius from axis = R_v + rho = 31.39 mm; width across any pair of parallel support lines = 60.0 mm exactly (tangency guarantees constant width; verify in Fusion with a driven dimension between two parallel tangent lines at several angles). Extrude symmetric or one-direction, T = 9.0 mm. BEARING: 608 (22 x 8 x 7 mm), open/unshielded, run dry. Central through-bore seat Ø21.90 mm for PLA (0.05 mm/side interference) or Ø21.80 mm for PETG, straight through the 9 mm body, with 0.5 mm x 45 deg lead-in chamfer on both ends. Print a test ring first (research-standard practice). 608 over R188 because: this is a 60 g printed body that needs the mass anyway, 608 FDM press-fit tolerances are the documented standard, and a 12.7 mm R188 seat has proportionally worse printed-bore error. WEIGHT POCKETS: 5 hex pockets for M8 DIN 934 steel nuts (13.0 mm AF, 6.5 mm high, ~5 g each), pocket across-flats = 13.3 mm (0.3 mm clearance), centered at radius r_p = 19.5 mm on the five arc-midpoint azimuths (36 deg offset from vertices, 72 deg apart), each rotated so one hex flat faces radially outward (maximizes rim wall; verify min wall to silhouette >= 1.6 mm). Pocket void spans z = 1.6 to 8.4 mm (floor 1.6 mm, depth 6.8 mm); ceiling z = 8.4-9.0 mm (3 x 0.2 mm layers bridging the 13.3 mm hex — within PLA bridge limits). FLICK DIMPLES: top face only, 5 spherical dimples Ø10 mm x 1.2 mm deep (sphere R = 11 mm), centered at r = 24.0 mm on the five VERTEX azimuths (interleaved with pockets, no overlap) — fingertip purchase at max moment arm without breaking the constant-width silhouette. EDGES: 1.2 mm x 45 deg chamfer on top and bottom perimeter edges (bottom chamfer also kills elephant's foot). GRIP CAPS (2x, printed): Ø20.0 mm disc, 3.3 mm thick, flat outer face with 0.8 x 45 deg rim chamfer; underside boss Ø11.0 x 0.7 mm (contacts inner race only, clearance recess beyond Ø12.5 mm so it never rubs the outer race); stem Ø8.10 x 3.0 mm press-fitting the 8 mm inner bore from each side (0.4 mm gap between stems mid-bore). Symmetry order: strict C5 rotational symmetry; all features on 72 deg patterns about the bearing axis.

**Mass Distribution:** Budget: PLA body ~18-19 g (100% infill, ~15 cm3 after bore/pockets/dimples), 5 x M8 steel nuts ~25 g, 608 bearing ~12 g, caps ~3 g; total ~58-60 g — inside the 60-70 g hand-feel sweet spot. Strategy: the five nuts are the dominant inertia contributors at r_p = 19.5 mm: I_nuts = 25 g x (1.95 cm)^2 ≈ 95 g·cm2; body shell adds ~65-75 g·cm2 (constant-width profile carries its area toward the rim better than a disc of equal width); total ≈ 165-180 g·cm2 — comparable to commercial metal spinners and roughly triple a bare-PLA print of the same size. Balance: C5 symmetry puts the CoM exactly on the bearing axis by construction; the only asymmetry (top-only dimples) is itself a 5-fold pattern, so it shifts CoM axially a fraction of a mm but never radially — no wobble. Deterministic slicer infill at 100% keeps the five sectors identical. Practical balance rule: use five nuts from the same batch/finish (nut-to-nut mass variance is the main imbalance source at 19.5 mm radius).

**Print Plan:** Material: PLA (primary; seat Ø21.90) or PETG (seat Ø21.80, pockets +0.1 mm AF). Orientation: flat bottom face on plate — the part is a pure 2D-profile extrusion, so zero supports: bore walls vertical, pocket walls vertical, 45 deg chamfers self-supporting, dimples face up, pocket ceilings are 13.3 mm bridges (safe). Settings: 0.2 mm layers, 0.4 mm nozzle, 4 perimeters, 100% infill (small part; guarantees balance and seat strength), first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed, 5 mm brim (small-footprint knock-off insurance), slicer elephant-foot compensation on. G-code pause at z = 8.4 mm: drop the five nuts in, resume; ceiling seals them captive. Caps print stem-up, flat face down, same settings, no supports. Tolerances: print the standard 608 test ring first and adjust seat by ±0.05 mm before the full 3-4 h body print; press bearing with a vise/clamp, never a hammer, chamfer leading. Bearing prep: pop shields if present, IPA soak, run dry.

**Why Fun:** Three sensations in one object. (1) Spin: ~170 g·cm2 of inertia on a clean dry 608 gives multi-minute spins with real gyroscopic push-back, and the constant-width outline does a magic trick — spinning it visually reads as a perfect circle, then the pentagon 'materializes' as it decays, giving a satisfying end-of-spin reveal. (2) Roll: constant width means it rolls between thumb and forefinger, or along a desk under your flat palm, with zero rise and fall — the same uncanny smoothness as rolling on Reuleaux rollers, a worry-stone mode no arm spinner has (the same property that lets the UK 50p coin work in coin slots). (3) Flick feel: five dimples means a flick landmark every 72 deg — more frequent than a tri-arm's 120 deg — and the smooth silhouette never snags a pocket.

**Why Unique:** Research shows Reuleaux triangles exist only as rock-in-frame fidgets, never as a spinner rotor profile — a constant-width spinner silhouette is open territory. It is simultaneously in the second underused class: odd 5-fold rotational symmetry, which the 2/3/6-arm market almost never uses. It reads instantly as different from every tri-arm/bar clone (no arms, no lobes, a mathematically exact curve), yet hides its differentiation honestly in geometry rather than reskinning. The dual-mode roll behavior is a functional feature no commercial or printed spinner archetype (bar, tri, quad, ring, coin) offers, and the spin-to-circle illusion is unique to constant-width outlines.

**Risks:** (1) CAD tangency errors: if the corner arcs and 57 mm side arcs are not exactly tangent (d must equal W-2*rho), the shape is not constant-width and the roll mode feels lumpy — constrain with tangent constraints, not eyeballed trims. (2) Seat cracking: PLA is brittle; over 0.05 mm/side interference or a missing lead-in chamfer can split the hub — mitigated by 4 perimeters, 100% infill, chamfer, vise press, and the mandatory test ring. (3) Bearing axial retention: a straight through-bore relies on friction alone; if the printed seat comes out loose the bearing can walk out — fall back to a drop of CA on the outer race (never the balls) or reprint at -0.05 mm. (4) Pocket ceiling sag: the 0.6 mm ceiling bridges 13.3 mm over steel; slight sag is cosmetic-only (internal), but if the pause height is misjudged the nozzle can strike a proud nut — nuts sit 0.3 mm below the resume layer by design; verify nut height before printing (some M8 nuts are 6.8 mm, which would collide — measure and adjust pocket depth parameter). (5) Nut mass variance between brands/batches causes rim imbalance and buzz — use five identical nuts. (6) Flick torque is friction-limited (dimples, not protruding arms), so spin-up needs 2-3 firm flicks — inherent to any smooth-silhouette rotor; the 72 deg dimple spacing mitigates it. (7) Wall between pocket and rim is ~1.6-2 mm at the thinnest azimuth — verify in CAD after any parameter change; below 1.2 mm it may delaminate under a drop.


## Design 1: Vernier Contra-Ring

**Direction:** Nested coaxial rings: print-in-place or assembled concentric rings that counter-rotate or shift visual pattern while spinning.

**Concept:** A two-rotor coaxial ring spinner: an inner slotted rotor rides a real 608 bearing at the center, and a heavy outer flywheel ring rides the inner rotor on a printed-in-place 45-degree diamond journal. Spun together (the default, because the steel bearing is far slipperier than the printed joint) it is a rim-weighted ring spinner with record-holder mass distribution; counter-spun by hand, the 36-slot and 30-window bands create a 6-node vernier/moire beat pattern that sweeps against the rotation.

**Geometry:** Overall: OD 86 mm (within 55-90 spec), uniform thickness Z = 8 mm, flat top and bottom (pure 2D-profile extrusion). Coordinate: R = radius from bearing axis, Z from build plate. Parameters for Fusion 360 (name = value): OD=86, T=8, seatD=21.9, boreD=8.0, hubOD=30, webRout=26.5, rimR=27.0, ridgeApexR=29.0, ringBoreR=27.5, ringOD_R=43, jointClr=0.5, slotN_A=36, slotN_B=30, chamf=0.5.

ROTOR A (inner rotor, one body): (1) Hub boss R0-15: through-bore for 608 bearing, seat dia 21.9 mm (22.0 nominal minus 0.05 mm/side PLA interference), bearing 7 mm wide centered in the 8 mm stack so both races sit 0.5 mm below each face; 0.5 x 45 deg lead-in chamfer on both seat mouths. (2) Slotted web R15-26.5, full 8 mm thick, with 36 radial through-slots 1.6 mm wide (tangential) running R16 to R25.5, equally spaced (10 deg pitch); web spokes between slots stay >= 1.2 mm at R16. Six dome nubs (3 mm dia, 1 mm tall) on the TOP face at R15, 60 deg pitch, as finger-drag pads. (3) Rim cylinder at R27.0, Z0-8, carrying the journal ridge: an annular isosceles-triangle protrusion with both faces at exactly 45 deg — base on the R27 cylinder from Z2.0 to Z6.0, apex circle at R29.0, Z4.0.

ROTOR B (outer flywheel ring, one body, printed in place around A): bore cylinder at R27.5 (0.5 mm radial gap to A's rim), Z0-8; the bore carries the mating diamond groove = rotor A's ridge profile offset 0.5 mm normal to every face (Fusion: offset the three ridge faces outward 0.5 mm; groove apex lands at ~R29.7, groove mouth spans ~Z1.3-6.7). Solid inner land R27.5-30. Vernier window band R30-39: 30 radial through-windows 2.2 mm wide, 12 deg pitch (webs ~4.1 mm at R30). Solid outer rim R39-43 (4 mm radial, the primary flywheel mass). OD has 12 scallop finger notches, 2 mm deep, 8 mm chord radius, 30 deg pitch, for flicking.

CAPS (2 identical, separate bodies): 20 mm dia, 4 mm thick, concave dished grip face 1.5 mm deep with rounded edge; underside has an 11.5 mm dia x 0.7 mm contact boss (presses only the 608 inner race, OD ~12 mm, clearing cage and outer race) and a central stem 8.1 mm dia x 3.4 mm long press-fitting the 8.0 mm bore from each side. Cap flange clears rotor A's face by 0.7 mm.

Symmetry orders: A = 36-fold (slots) x 6-fold (nubs); B = 30-fold (windows) x 12-fold (scallops); all concentric, CoM on axis by construction. All bottom edges (both rotors, both bore mouths, groove mouth) get 0.5 x 45 deg chamfers.

**Mass Distribution:** Strategy: rim ring I = mr^2, the record-holder layout. At 100% infill PETG (1.27 g/cm3): rotor B ~ 29 g with over 60% of its mass in the solid R39-43 rim (mean radius ~36 mm) -> I_B ~ 3.8e-5 kg m^2 alone. Rotor A ~ 15 g (hub + slotted web, low radius), steel 608 ~ 12 g on-axis (adds heft, negligible I), caps ~ 4 g. Total ~ 60-65 g at 86 mm — inside the 60-70 g sweet spot, and total I ~ 4.5e-5 kg m^2, roughly 2-3x a typical 52 mm/68 g commercial spinner, so spin time and gyroscopic push-back beat the norm without any metal inserts. Balance: every feature array is a full n-fold circular pattern about the bearing axis, slicer infill is deterministic and the part is 100% infill, so per-slice CoM stays on axis; the only asymmetry (6 top-face nubs, ~0.1 g total) is rotationally balanced and axially negligible. In locked mode both rotors co-rotate on the 608 (printed-joint friction >> bearing friction, so the pair behaves as one flywheel); braking rotor A with a thumb on the nubs while B coasts gives a differential 'inertia clutch' feel unique to the two-rotor stack.

**Print Plan:** Orientation: flat on the plate, one plate, no supports anywhere. Rotors A+B print as ONE print-in-place job (the diamond joint is captive); caps print alongside, flat back down, dish up. Material: PLA preferred (crisper joint, use jointClr = 0.5 mm per PLA rotating-clearance data); for PETG increase jointClr to 0.6 mm (PETG fuses at PLA clearances). Settings: 0.2 mm layers, 0.4 mm nozzle, 4 perimeters, 100% infill (part is small; perimeters+solid infill also keep balance deterministic), seam set to 'random' or rear-aligned so seam blobs do not unbalance a rotor or ridge the journal, first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed, 5 mm brim optional (86 mm footprint usually holds), elephant-foot compensation ON plus the designed 0.5 x 45 chamfers on all bottom edges. Overhang audit: joint ridge and groove faces are exactly 45 deg (printable both parts); every other surface is vertical or flat-top; zero bridges. Tolerances: bearing seat modeled at 21.9 mm — print the included test ring (10 mm tall hub coupon with identical seat + chamfer) first, adjust seatD in one 0.05 mm step if needed, since printed holes come out undersized and vary with calibration. Assembly: break the print-in-place joint free by twisting the rings before installing the bearing; press the 608 with a vise (never hammer) through the chamfered mouth; press caps in last. Run the bearing dry and open (degrease in IPA) for 2-5+ minute locked-mode spins. Print time ~3.5 h, ~55 g filament.

**Why Fun:** Three distinct play modes from one object. (1) Locked mode: flick the scalloped rim and the whole 60+ g, rim-weighted ring spins on the dry 608 with strong gyroscopic push-back and long spin — the ring archetype both world records used. (2) Contra mode: pinch the caps, spin the outer ring, then drag the inner rotor's nubs the other way — the 36-slot and 30-window bands beat against each other and 6 ghost spokes sweep visibly opposite to your fingers, a genuine counter-rotating moire illusion with zero electronics. (3) Clutch mode: brake the inner rotor mid-spin and feel the outer flywheel coast on, then re-engage — a tactile differential sensation no single-rotor spinner can produce. Slow relative rotation also gives a satisfying vernier-caliper alignment crawl between the two slot counts.

**Why Unique:** The market is ~half tri-arm clones plus bar spinners; nested-ring prints that exist are gimbal gyros (rings tumbling on perpendicular axes) or bearingless print-in-place toys. This design occupies the documented gap: coaxial nested rings that counter-rotate about the SAME axis, combining a real 608 bearing with a print-in-place journal — the research notes that pairing gimbal/nested rings WITH a true bearing spinner is rare, and counter-rotating moire grids driven by finger spin were flagged as novel. The vernier beat (36 vs 30) is a deliberate engineered illusion, not surface ornament, and the dual-friction architecture (steel bearing << printed journal) is what makes one flick default to 'spin together' while still allowing counter-rotation on demand. No commercial or mainstream printed spinner offers a two-rotor differential feel.

**Risks:** 1) Joint fusing: if first layers over-squish, the 0.5 mm diamond clearance can weld — mitigated by elephant-foot compensation, the designed bottom chamfers, and breaking the joint free before bearing install; PETG needs 0.6 mm or it will fuse. 2) Journal friction/roughness: layer seams inside the groove ridge the bearing surface — random seam placement and a short break-in spin are required; contra-mode spins of the outer ring will decay in tens of seconds, not minutes (expectation: contra mode is the visual/tactile toy, locked mode is the long spin). 3) PLA seat cracking: 0.05 mm/side interference is the ceiling for brittle PLA; vise-press through the chamfer, and the test-ring step is mandatory because printed bores run undersized and calibration-dependent. 4) Balance sensitivity: a partial-infill slice or clustered seam on one rotor shows up as buzz at speed — 100% infill and random seams are load-bearing settings, not suggestions. 5) 86 mm OD is at the top of the comfortable range and pockets poorly; scale-to-75 mm variant possible but loses ~35% of I. 6) Slot top edges are crisp; add optional 0.3 mm top chamfers if fingertip drag across the web feels sharp.


## Design 2: Maelstrom Reticle

**Direction:** Zoetrope/moire visual effect: geometry patterned so spinning produces an animated illusion (moire fringes, phenakistoscope frames) at flick speeds.

**Concept:** A 71 mm armless ring spinner that turns the bearing's own stator/rotor split into an optical instrument: a slitted reticle disc rides on the stationary pinch-grip cap, floating 0.8 mm above a 6-start spiral relief on the spinning rotor face. Viewed through the reticle, the spiral becomes a flowing expand/contract moire animation whose speed is a live readout of spin decay — a zoetrope with no strobe, no electronics, and no second spinner, generated entirely between your fingertips.

**Geometry:** BEARING: 608 (22 OD x 8 bore x 7 mm). Justified over R188 because (a) the 8 mm bore accepts stiff printed stub axles that must cantilever the reticle disc — an R188's 6.35 mm bore leaves marginal printed-stub stiffness at 0.4 mm nozzle resolution; (b) at 71 mm OD the design has the mass budget a 608 wants; (c) it is the de facto standard for print-file spinners. Run open/dry.

ROTOR (one piece, defines in-use z: 0 = bottom face, 9 = top/pattern face; all diameters parametric):
- Overall: OD 71.0 mm (R_out = 35.5), height 9.0 mm, full rotational symmetry (no arms).
- Hub: annulus r11.0 to r15.5, z 0 to 9. Bearing seat: bore Ø21.85 (PETG; Ø21.90 for PLA — 0.05-0.075 mm/side interference per research), depth 7.0 mm from z=0, 0.5 x 45 deg lead-in chamfer at the mouth. Retaining shoulder z 7 to 9, shoulder inner Ø19.5 (bears on outer race only; annular ceiling bridge span ~1.2 mm).
- Web: annulus r15.5 to r29.0, z 5.8 to 9.0 (3.2 mm thick, flush with top face). Open recess below faces downward in use.
- Pattern (engraved into top face z=9): 6-start Archimedean spiral, each start r = 15.5 + (36/2pi)*theta mm (36 mm radial advance per revolution; 6 starts give 6 mm radial line spacing), groove width 2.0 mm, depth 0.8 mm, swept over annulus r16 to r29. Optional filament swap at the last 0.6 mm for two-color contrast.
- Rim: annulus r29.0 to r35.5, z 0 to 9. Internal annular BB channel: r29.8 to r34.6 (4.8 mm wide), z 2.0 to 7.0 (5 mm tall); inner wall 0.8 mm, outer wall 0.9 mm, floor/ceiling 2 mm. Holds ~44 loose 4.5 mm steel BBs (airsoft/slingshot standard).
- Rim top face (z=9), annulus r30 to r35: 36 radial tick grooves, 1.2 mm wide x 0.5 mm deep (stroboscopic layer — appears to freeze near 2.8 rev/s under 100 Hz mains-LED flicker). Rim OD: 36 finger scallops, R2.0 mm, for flicking.
- 0.5 x 45 deg chamfer on every bottom and top outer edge.

RETICLE (flat part): disc Ø66 (r33), 1.6 mm thick; central boss Ø14, total boss height 4.0 mm incl. disc, through-bore Ø8.05; 41 radial slit apertures (prime — avoids harmonic lock with the 6-fold spiral and 36 rim ticks) from r15 to r31, 50% duty (slit and spoke each 4.39 deg; min spoke width 1.15 mm at r15); solid tie rings r11-r15 and r31-r33.

TOP CAP: button Ø20 x 4 mm, chamfered edge, three concentric 0.6 mm-deep grip grooves on the face; stub Ø8.0 x 10.3 mm (distal 3.5 mm engages the inner race; passes freely through reticle boss and shoulder opening). BOTTOM CAP: button Ø20 x 4 mm, same face; 0.3 mm-tall Ø11 standoff boss; stub Ø8.0 x 3.3 mm. Stubs abut mid-race with 0.2 mm clearance. Print Ø7.9/8.0/8.1 test pins to calibrate race fit.

ASSEMBLY STACK: bottom cap into inner race from below; bearing pressed into seat from z=0 (vise, not hammer); reticle (boss up) over top cap stub, clamped between button underside and boss; stub into inner race. Reticle underside sits at z=9.8 — 0.8 mm air gap above the pattern face. Total assembled height ~21.8 mm.

MOIRE BEHAVIOR: spiral seen through static radial slits yields circular fringes flowing radially at 36 mm x rev/s (waterfall at flick speed, resolving to a slow hypnotic expansion during spin-down; reversed flick direction gives contraction; stopping triggers the documented motion aftereffect).

**Mass Distribution:** Ring archetype — the physically optimal form per both world-record spinners (I = mr^2 for a rim vs mr^2/2 for a disc). Mass budget ~56 g total: 44 steel BBs (~16 g) at mean r=32.2 mm contribute ~1.7e-5 kg·m^2; PETG rim (~9 g at r~32) adds ~0.9e-5; thin 3.2 mm web keeps mid-radius mass low; hub is minimal. Total I ~ 3.1e-5 kg·m^2 — rim-dominated, comparable to quality commercial spinners, with the 608's 12 g dead mass at the center accepted as the price of stub stiffness. Balance: every rotating feature is n-fold symmetric (6-start spiral, 36 ticks/scallops); the 41-slit asymmetry-adjacent count lives on the NON-rotating reticle so it cannot unbalance the rotor. The loose-BB channel doubles as a Leblanc-style passive auto-balancer: above the critical speed the balls migrate to counteract residual print imbalance (deterministic slicer infill plus symmetric geometry keeps static imbalance small to begin with). Pack the channel to ~98% (44 of 45 capacity) so migration is limited to fine correction rather than clumping.

**Print Plan:** Material: PETG preferred (tolerant press-fit, non-brittle seat); PLA acceptable with seat opened to Ø21.90. Four parts, all support-free. ROTOR: printed pattern-face DOWN on a smooth PEI plate — this makes the spiral grooves crisp first-layer outlines, turns the web recess into an upward-facing (unsupported-OK) opening, and opens the bearing seat upward so it needs no bridging; only bridges are the 4.8 mm BB-channel ceiling and the 1.2 mm seat shoulder, both within the 5-10 mm reliable window. 0.2 mm layers (0.16 optional for pattern crispness), 4 perimeters (>=3 required at the seat), 40% gyroid infill, 5 mm brim, elephant-foot compensation ~0.15 mm (pattern grooves are on the bed face — compensation is load-bearing here). G-code pause at print-z = 7.0 mm to drop in 44 BBs before the channel ceiling closes. RETICLE: flat on bed, boss up, 0.2 mm layers, 3 perimeters, brim (thin disc, warp-prone — cool bed for PETG). CAPS: grip face on bed, stub up (vertical Ø8 pin, well above the 2 mm minimum pin size). Tolerances: seat Ø21.85 PETG / Ø21.90 PLA with 0.5 x 45 lead-in chamfer, pressed with a vise; print a bearing test ring and the 7.9/8.0/8.1 stub test pins first (printed holes run undersized and calibration-dependent per research). Clean the 608 with IPA and run dry.

**Why Fun:** Three stacked illusions in one object, each tuned to a different speed regime: at flick speed the reticle turns the spiral into a rushing radial waterfall; as spin decays the flow slows into a hypnotic breathing expansion — making the normally boring spin-down the best part and giving a live analog speedometer; under ordinary mains-LED lighting the 36 rim ticks strobe-freeze around 2.8 rev/s with no electronics. Reversing flick direction flips expansion to contraction, and staring then stopping triggers a genuine motion aftereffect. Underneath the optics it still feels premium: a smooth, heavy, gyroscopically alive steel-shot ring with scalloped grip and no arm-pulse.

**Why Unique:** No published design mounts an optical reticle on the stationary grip cap — existing zoetrope spinners (Odom's cat-run) need an external strobe or camera shutter, moire prints need two hand-held counter-rotating discs, and stroboscopic tops need flickering light. This is the only architecture where the sampling grid and the phase pattern are both onboard and permanently registered by the bearing itself, so the illusion works in steady daylight. Secondary differentiators: armless ring silhouette (tri-arm clones are ~half the market; rings are the record-holding but underused form), a BB-filled rim that doubles as a passive auto-balancer (unclaimed in print-file spinners), and a bonus mains-LED strobe layer. Every mechanism is grounded in documented effects: slit sampling (zoetrope), spiral expansion illusion, moire between relatively rotating gratings, and Leblanc ball balancing.

**Risks:** (1) Seat cracking on press-fit, especially PLA — mitigated by 0.05 mm/side interference, 4 perimeters, lead-in chamfer, vise pressing, and a mandatory test ring. (2) Stub-to-inner-race fit is calibration-sensitive: too loose and the cap (and reticle) pulls out, too tight splits the stub — hence the three-pin test coupon. (3) Moire strength is sensitive to the 0.8 mm air gap; if reticle warp increases the gap or causes rub, the effect washes out or the reticle drags — brim, cool-bed PETG settings, and the stiff Ø14 boss mitigate; worst case, sand the reticle rim ring. (4) Loose BBs can clump below the auto-balancing critical speed, adding low-speed wobble and rattle — near-full 98% packing limits migration; a silent variant packs the channel 100% or replaces BBs with printed density via 100% infill (costing ~40% of rim inertia). (5) Elephant foot can smear the bed-face spiral grooves — compensation must be enabled and first layer dialed in. (6) The strobe-freeze layer depends on ambient LED flicker (100/120 Hz); in flicker-free lighting only the moire effects show. (7) Reticle blocks direct view of the spiral when removed from context — users who want a naked spinner can simply omit the reticle; the spinner functions normally without it.


## Design 3: Reuleaux Seven

**Direction:** High-order symmetry: 5-fold or 7-fold rotational symmetry (pentagonal/heptagonal), which almost no market spinner uses, with rim-weighted coin pockets.

**Concept:** A 7-fold rotationally symmetric spinner whose silhouette is a rounded Reuleaux heptagon — the constant-width shape of the UK 50p coin — with seven sealed coin pockets ringing the rim. It fuses two geometry classes the research shows are essentially unclaimed (odd 5/7-fold symmetry and Reuleaux rotor profiles) into one body, and gains a second fidget mode for free: being constant-width, it rolls smoothly between two fingers or across a desk at constant height like a 50p, even when not spinning.

**Geometry:** Parametric spec (Fusion 360 user parameters in brackets). PROFILE: rounded Reuleaux heptagon of constant width [W]=78.0 mm. Construct 7 vertex points on a circle of radius Rv = W/(2*cos(pi/14)) = 40.01 mm, spaced 360/7 = 51.4286 deg. Each edge is an arc of radius [W]-[Rtip]=75.0 mm centered on the OPPOSITE vertex; each vertex is rounded with a [Rtip]=3.0 mm arc centered on that vertex point (this offset construction preserves exact constant width W = 75+3 = 78). Max dimension across = 2*Rv = 80.0 mm; inradius = W - Rv = 37.99 mm. BODY: extrude the profile [T]=8.0 mm. Chamfer top outer edge 1.0 x 45 deg (cosmetic/grip), bottom outer edge 0.5 x 45 deg (elephant-foot). BEARING SEAT (608, press-fit from top): counterbore dia [Dseat]=21.90 mm (PLA; use 21.80 for PETG) x 7.10 mm deep, concentric with the profile centroid; below it a through-hole dia 19.0 mm forming a 0.9 mm retaining lip at the bottom face; 0.5 x 45 deg lead-in chamfer at the top of the counterbore. Bearing (22 OD x 8 ID x 7 wide) seats on the lip, top face ~1.0 mm below the body top. COIN POCKETS: 7 cylindrical pockets, dia [Dpocket] = coin dia + 0.30 mm = 20.62 mm (UK 1p, 20.32 x 1.65 mm, 3.56 g; set 19.35 for US pennies), depth [Ppock]=3.40 mm (two stacked 1p + 0.10 clearance; set 1.85 for single-coin light build). Pocket floors at Z=1.20; pocket centers at radius [Rc]=26.0 mm, one aligned under each vertex (circular pattern, 7x). Wall checks at these values: pocket-to-pocket web 2*26*sin(pi/7) - 20.62 = 1.94 mm; pocket-to-rim at inradius 37.99 - (26+10.31) = 1.68 mm; pocket-to-bearing-bore 15.69 - 10.95 = 4.74 mm — all at or above the 1.6 mm (4-extrusion) floor. GRIP CAPS (x2, printed): dia 16.0 mm disc, 4.5 mm tall, concave dish (spherical radius 25 mm, 1.5 mm deep) on the outer face, dia 7.95 x 3.3 mm stub press-fitting the bearing's 8 mm inner race, 0.4 x 45 deg chamfer on stub tip and dish rim. Symmetry order: strict C7 for the rotor; CoM is on the bearing axis by construction.

**Mass Distribution:** Coins are the flywheel: 14x UK 1p (49.8 g of steel/copper) at pocket-center radius 26 mm contribute ~ 49.8 g x 26^2 = 33,700 g-mm^2 plus ~2,600 g-mm^2 about their own axes — the dominant term, per I = sum(m r^2). PLA body (~31 g at 100% infill, net volume ~25.5 cm^3) adds roughly 18,000 g-mm^2 because the Reuleaux annulus keeps most plastic outboard of the pockets; hub is hollowed by the 22 mm bearing bore. Total I ~ 54,000 g-mm^2, comparable to premium metal bars. Total mass ~96 g (body 31 + coins 50 + 608 bearing 12 + caps 3) in the two-coin build — deliberately at the heavy end for long spins and strong gyroscopic push-back; the single-coin parameter drops it to ~71 g for the 60-90 g EDC sweet spot. Balance: C7 symmetry puts CoM on axis exactly; identical-denomination coins (mint tolerance well under 0.1 g) and deterministic slicer infill keep per-lobe inertia matched, and 7 lobes tolerate small per-lobe error far better than a 2-arm bar. The 608 choice is justified by scale: at 80 mm across, the 22 mm OD occupies only 28% of the diameter, the coin ring supplies rim mass the bearing footprint can't steal, its 8 mm bore takes simple printed grip caps, and a 0.05 mm/side FDM press fit on a 22 mm seat is proven practice (an R188 would demand a 12.7 mm seat where FDM tolerance is a much larger fraction of the fit). Spec an open, dry, hybrid-ceramic 608 for best decay constant.

**Print Plan:** Orientation: flat bottom face on the build plate — the body is a pure 2D-profile extrusion, so zero supports. All external non-vertical geometry is 45 deg chamfers; pocket ceilings are formed by resuming over the inserted coins. Material: PETG preferred (tougher press fit, 0.05-0.10 mm/side interference tolerance); PLA acceptable with the 21.90 mm seat and vise-press only. Layer height 0.20 mm; 4 perimeters; 100% infill (or minimum 4 walls + 50% gyroid — deterministic infill preserves balance); first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed with a 5 mm brim; slicer elephant-foot compensation on. Coin embedding: G-code pause at Z=4.60 mm (pocket floor 1.20 + depth 3.40), drop two coins per pocket, resume; the first resumed layer lands on the coin faces with ~0.1 mm gap, so the nominal 20.6 mm 'bridge' is actually fully coin-supported — no real bridging occurs (well under the 10 mm free-bridge guideline). Bearing seat: 3+ perimeters and solid infill locally, 0.5 x 45 lead-in chamfer, press with a vise. Tolerances: print the standard 608 test ring first and adjust [Dseat] by the measured deviation before committing to the 3-4 h body print. Caps print dish-up, stub-down on a brim. Assembly: press bearing from top until seated on the lip, push caps into the inner race from both sides.

**Why Fun:** Seven vertices means a flick landmark every 51.4 deg — the densest flick cadence of any common format (tri = 120 deg), so spin-up is effortless despite the high inertia, and any catch angle lands near a corner for braking. At ~96 g with a rim-dominated I, angular momentum is high: the gyroscopic precession push-back when you tilt it is pronounced and long-lived, and spins in the multi-minute range are realistic with a clean dry bearing. The odd symmetry order gives a distinctive visual: under mains/LED flicker the 7 lobes produce wagon-wheel shimmer effects that even-count spinners don't. And the constant-width silhouette is a party trick in itself — roll it under a flat hand or along a table and it translates perfectly smoothly with no rise and fall, exactly like the 50p coin it quotes, giving a second, silent desk-fidget mode.

**Why Unique:** The research pack identifies odd 5/7-fold rotational symmetry as genuinely rare (market is 2/3/6-arm; only a couple of obscure 'Quintuple'/'PentaFlex' prints exist) and Reuleaux profiles as used only for rock-in-frame fidgets, never as a spinner rotor silhouette. This design claims both open niches simultaneously and adds a cultural hook — the UK 50p is the world's most recognizable Reuleaux heptagon, and coin pockets filled with pennies inside a coin-shaped rotor is a legible joke. Unlike tri-arm clones it has no arms at all: it reads as a solid heptagonal medallion, closer to the physically optimal ring/disc class (both world-record spinners were rims) but with the grip landmarks rings lack. No commercial or mainstream printed spinner combines constant-width geometry, 7-fold symmetry, and sealed rim coin ballast.

**Risks:** 1) Thin webs: the 1.94 mm pocket-to-pocket and 1.68 mm pocket-to-rim walls are near the printable floor; a slicer set to 5+ perimeters could fail to fill them — verify in slicer preview, and if [W] is reduced below 78 the walls collapse (parameter guard: keep Rc <= inradius - 11.9 and 2*Rc*sin(pi/7) >= Dpocket + 1.6). 2) Press-fit cracking: PLA around the 22 mm seat can split, aggravated by the pockets 4.7 mm away — mitigate with PETG, the lead-in chamfer, vise pressing, and the test-ring calibration step. 3) Pause-at-height dependency: forgetting the pause or a printer that purges/shifts on resume ruins the embed; fallback is an open-pocket variant with 7 printed press-in lids. 4) Coin variance: worn coinage or mixed pre/post-1992 1p compositions (3.56 vs 3.56 g nominal but different wear) can introduce slight imbalance; use same-era coins. 5) Weight: 96 g exceeds the 60-90 g guide range — hand fatigue for some users; the single-coin parameter is the documented remedy. 6) 80 mm across-corners is at the top of the pocketable range and may snag fabric; this is a desk-first spinner. 7) Elephant foot narrowing the bottom of the bearing bore lip — compensation plus the bottom chamfer must both be enabled.


## Design 4: Quoin

**Direction:** Superellipse/squircle profile: smooth constant-feel silhouette between circle and square, rim-weighted, minimal snag.

**Concept:** A single-body squircle spinner: a superellipse (n=4) coin-like rotor whose four gently swelling corners sit 18.9% further from the axis than its flats and each carry a sealed-in M8 steel nut. It reads as a smooth pocket coin, not a spinner — fully convex silhouette, zero snag points — yet spins like a rim-weighted quad because the weights sit exactly at the profile's radius maxima. Flicking is done by rolling a fingertip off the swelling corners, which arrive every 90 degrees.

**Geometry:** PLAN PROFILE: superellipse |x/a|^n + |y/a|^n = 1 with n = 4, a = 30 mm. Parametric form for Fusion 360 fitted spline (sample t every 5 deg, close spline): x = a*sgn(cos t)*|cos t|^(2/n), y = a*sgn(sin t)*|sin t|^(2/n). Flat-to-flat width 60 mm; diagonal (max) diameter 2*a*2^(1/4) = 71.35 mm; radius varies 30.00 -> 35.68 mm, 4-fold symmetric (C4v). BODY: flat extrusion of that profile, thickness T = 10.0 mm (z = 0 to 10). Outer edge: 2.0 mm x 45 deg chamfer on both top and bottom silhouette edges (bottom chamfer also kills elephant-foot contact; both print cleanly at 45 deg). BEARING SEAT (608, 22 OD x 8 ID x 7 mm): stepped coaxial bore. From bottom face: Ø19.5 mm through z = 0 to 1.5 (retaining ledge ring that contacts only the outer race face); from z = 1.5 to 10: Ø21.9 mm for PLA (0.05 mm/side interference) or Ø21.8 mm for PETG (0.10 mm/side). 0.5 x 45 deg lead-in chamfer at the top bore edge; 0.5 x 45 deg chamfer on the bottom Ø19.5 opening. Bearing pressed from top, seats on ledge at z = 1.5-8.5, so bearing mid-plane = body mid-plane = z 5.0 (no static tilt moment). Model a solid annulus Ø22-Ø28 around the seat (or force >=4 perimeters there) per anti-crack guidance. WEIGHT POCKETS: 4 hexagonal prism pockets on the diagonals at 45/135/225/315 deg, pocket-center radius r_w = 24.0 mm. Hex across-flats 13.3 mm (M8 DIN 934 nut AF 13.0 + 0.3 clearance), all four hexes clocked identically with one flat-pair aligned to the print X axis so ceiling bridges span 13.3 mm across flats. Pocket z = 1.6 to 8.6 (depth 7.0 for the 6.8 mm nut), floor 1.6 mm, sealed ceiling 1.4 mm (7 layers at 0.2). Vertical pocket corners filleted r = 0.5 mm (stress relief). Minimum web: pocket outer corner to skin >= 2.3 mm at the diagonal; pocket inner edge (16.8 mm) to seat annulus (14 mm) = 2.8 mm. GRIP CAPS (2 off, printed): head Ø23 x 3.5 mm with 0.6 mm edge roundover; concave grip dimple = 45 deg conical countersink, Ø16 mouth, 1.5 mm deep, centered in head face; stem Ø8.10 x 3.3 mm long pressing into the bearing's 8.0 mm ID from each side (stems do not touch; 0.4 mm axial gap). Fusion parameter list: n=4, a=30, T=10, seat_d=21.9, ledge_d=19.5, ledge_t=1.5, nut_af=13.3, nut_depth=7.0, pocket_floor=1.6, r_w=24, cap_head=23, stem_d=8.1.

**Mass Distribution:** Target total ~65 g: printed body 27-30 g (PLA, 4 perimeters + 40% gyroid infill — slicer infill is deterministic and 4-fold symmetric geometry keeps per-quadrant inertia identical), 4x M8 steel nuts 21.6 g, 608 bearing 12 g, caps ~3 g. The nuts sit at 24 mm center radius, precisely under the profile's radius maxima: I_nuts ~ 21.6 g x (24 mm)^2 ~ 12,400 g-mm^2, roughly equal to the entire body's ~12,600 g-mm^2, so half the moment of inertia comes from 1/3 of the mass placed at the corners — rim-weighting hidden inside a smooth coin form. Estimated total I ~ 2.5e-5 kg-m^2, comparable to a 68 g commercial spinner. Balance approach: strict C4v symmetry puts CoM on the bearing axis by construction; bearing mid-plane coincides with body mid-plane (no wobble couple); nuts are the only variable — use four same-batch nuts and check they match within ~0.1 g on a kitchen/jewelry scale before the pause-insert. Any residual nut-mass spread sits at only 24 mm radius across four positions, so imbalance sensitivity is lower than a 2-arm bar carrying the same mass.

**Print Plan:** Body prints flat on the plate — pure 2D-profile extrusion, no supports anywhere. Material: PETG preferred (tougher seat, 0.10 mm/side interference, use Ø21.8 seat) or PLA (Ø21.9 seat). 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, 4 perimeters, 40% gyroid (or 100% for maximum heft), first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed, 5 mm brim optional (footprint is large and convex, so adhesion is easy; no sharp plan corners to curl — minimum plan curvature radius is at the corners and is generous). Slicer elephant-foot compensation on, plus the modeled 0.5 mm bottom chamfers. G-code pause at Z = 8.6 mm: drop the four weighed nuts in, resume; the 1.4 mm ceiling bridges 13.3 mm across the hex flats (above the 5-10 mm ideal but well inside PLA/PETG bridge capability; sag is hidden inside the sealed pocket). All internal bridged spans are flat hex ceilings — no curved unsupported ceilings. Bearing: 608, open/unshielded, run dry (IPA-cleaned); print a 6 mm-tall test ring with the same seat geometry first and press-fit-check before committing the 3-4 h body print; press with a vise against the outer race only — the ledge takes the load. Caps print grip-face down: the 45 deg conical dimple prints as a self-supporting bottom recess; stem points up (Ø8.1 x 3.3 mm, well above minimum pin size).

**Why Fun:** The feel is the point: a fully convex rotor gives a smooth, lobeless silhouette in the palm, but the 18.9% radial swell means a corner sweeps under your fingertip every 90 degrees — a soft quad pulse rather than a tri-arm's chop. Because the swell is gradual there is nothing to catch, so you can cage it loosely between thumb and finger while it runs, feeling the four heartbeats per revolution and the gyroscopic push-back from ~2.5e-5 kg-m^2 of corner-loaded inertia. It flick-starts every 90 degrees (four landmarks beats a bar's two), brakes progressively by squeezing the smooth rim, and pockets like a fat coin with zero fabric snag.

**Why Unique:** Research shows the superellipse exists in fidget-space only as a stacked sliding fidget (MakerWorld 538174) — never as a spinner silhouette; the market is ~half tri-arm clones and the rest reskins. Quoin occupies the unclaimed gap between coin/disc (discreet but low I per gram) and quad-arm (high I but snaggy and bulky): it is a disc-class object with arm-class mass placement, because the squircle's corners ARE the max-radius points. No visible arms, no concavities, 4-fold symmetry instead of the ubiquitous 3-fold, and the weights are invisible — sealed at print time — so the finished object looks like a machined pebble, not a fidget toy. Choice of 608 over R188 is deliberate for FDM: the 22 mm seat is a proven printed press-fit with published tolerances, the design has ample mass budget (the research's 608 penalty — body volume — is irrelevant when the profile already surrounds it with 30 g of plastic and 22 g of steel), and R188's 12.7 mm OD seat plus tiny caps are much less tolerant of FDM hole-undersizing.

**Risks:** 1) Bearing seat cracking in PLA if the printer prints the bore tight — mitigated by 0.05 mm/side conservative interference, lead-in chamfer, solid annulus around the seat, and the mandatory test-ring coupon; PETG variant is more forgiving. 2) Printed-hole undersizing varies by machine, so the seat may end up loose instead of tight — the test ring catches this; fallback is a drop of CA on the outer race. 3) 13.3 mm pocket-ceiling bridges will sag a little on poorly cooled machines — cosmetic only (sealed pocket), but severe sag could rub a nut; keep part cooling at 100% for those layers. 4) Nut mass variance or a missed pause causes buzz/wobble — weigh nuts, and if the user skips the pause the spinner still works, just ~21 g lighter and shorter-spinning. 5) The gentle 4-lobe feel has a shorter flick moment arm (35.7 mm max radius, no deep finger notch) than an armed spinner, so reaching top speed takes 2-3 flicks — inherent to the minimal-snag direction, partially offset by the chamfered edge giving a fingernail catch. 6) Elephant foot could shrink the bottom Ø19.5 ledge opening — chamfered and slicer-compensated; the opening is non-critical clearance, not a fit.


## Design 5: Maelstrom P5

**Direction:** Spiral/involute arms: logarithmic-spiral arm forms that are rotationally balanced, look like motion even at rest, and blur into a disc when spun.

**Concept:** A five-armed logarithmic-spiral spinner that reads as a frozen whirlpool: each arm is a constant-pitch log spiral sweeping 110 degrees from hub to a weighted tip bulb, so the silhouette implies rotation even sitting still. Spun, the five spiral edges and engraved top-face spiral grooves merge into a disc with a documented expand/contract motion illusion. Strict C5 rotational symmetry keeps the center of mass exactly on the bearing axis despite the dynamic, "unbalanced-looking" form.

**Geometry:** Global: OD 72.0 mm, uniform thickness 8.0 mm, flat top/bottom (pure 2D-profile extrusion), 5-fold rotational symmetry (circular pattern, 72 deg). Fusion 360 parameters: N=5; OD=72; t=8; r0=13.0; b=0.5007; sweep=110deg; armOffset=34deg; bulbDia=15.0; bulbR=28.5.

Hub: Ø30.0 mm boss. Bearing = 608 (22x8x7 mm), chosen because: (a) the printed body plus steel nut weights need a bearing that tolerates FDM seat error — 0.1 mm on a 22 mm seat is a smaller relative error than on an R188's 12.7 mm; (b) rim mass comes from dense steel inserts, not body volume, so the 608's footprint costs nothing; (c) its 7 mm width nearly fills the 8 mm body for a flush look. Seat: from bottom face a Ø20.6 mm through-hole; from top face a Ø21.90 mm counterbore 7.1 mm deep (PLA interference 0.05 mm/side; use Ø21.80 for PETG), leaving a 0.9 mm bottom shoulder that contacts only the outer-race land (20.6–21.9 annulus). 0.5 x 45 deg lead-in chamfer at the counterbore mouth. Printed flat, the shoulder is solid first layers — no bridging.

Arms (x5): leading edge is the logarithmic spiral r(theta) = 13.0 * e^(0.5007*theta), theta 0 to 1.9199 rad (110 deg), drawn CCW (equation-driven curve or 15-point spline in Fusion). Trailing edge is the identical spiral rotated +34 deg about the origin. Constant pitch angle ~63.4 deg between tangent and radius gives the "motion at rest" look. Spiral terminus r = 13.0*e^(0.5007*1.9199) = 34.0 mm. Tip bulb: Ø15.0 mm circle centered at r = 28.5 mm on the arm's end angle; both spiral edges blend tangentially into the bulb; outer extent 28.5 + 7.5 = 36.0 mm = OD/2. Fillets: R4 where edges meet the Ø30 hub, R3 at concave edge-to-bulb junctions, R2 minimum on the trailing cusp (no sharp plan-view points). Between-arm windows are open.

Weight pockets (x5): hex pocket for M8 steel nut (13.0 mm across-flats, 6.5 mm thick, ~4.9 g), pocket 13.25 mm AF x 6.7 mm deep from the top face, centered on each bulb center (r=28.5 mm), one flat tangential; 1.3 mm solid floor remains.

Grip caps (x2, printed): Ø20.0 mm flange, 2.5 mm thick, concave dish (R40 spherical cut, 1.5 mm deep), stem Ø8.10 x 3.2 mm pressing into the bearing's 8 mm bore from each side (stems total 6.4 mm inside the 7 mm bore, never touching); a raised Ø11 x 0.5 mm ring under the flange contacts only the inner race.

Surface graphics: on the top face, three 0.8 mm wide x 0.4 mm deep engraved grooves per arm-gap, same-b log spirals phase-shifted 8 deg apart, running from hub edge to rim — these create the spinning-spiral optical effect. Edges: 0.5 x 45 deg chamfer on every bottom edge (elephant's foot), 1.0 x 45 deg chamfer on top perimeter edges for finger feel.

**Mass Distribution:** Rim-first budget per I = sum(m*r^2). Five M8 steel nuts (~24.5 g total) sit at r = 28.5 mm: I_nuts ~ 2.0e-5 kg*m^2. PLA body at 100% infill ~22 g (net volume ~17.8 cm^3 after bore and pockets), itself rim-biased because the spiral arms thicken into Ø15 bulbs at max radius: I_body ~ 0.7e-5. Total rotor I ~ 2.8e-5 kg*m^2 — roughly 2.5-3x a plain printed tri-arm, from steel at 79% of max radius. Total mass ~61 g (body 22 + nuts 24.5 + 608 bearing ~12 + caps ~3), inside the 60-70 g hand-feel sweet spot at 72 mm OD. Balance is by construction: exact C5 symmetry puts CoM on axis regardless of the asymmetric-looking sweep; identical arms plus deterministic slicer paths at 100% infill keep per-arm inertia equal. The only balance-critical user step is fitting five same-batch nuts (mass variation between nut brands is the main imbalance source).

**Print Plan:** Orientation: flat on the build plate, engraved top face up. Zero supports — the part is a straight extrusion; the bearing shoulder prints as bottom solid layers and all chamfers are 45 deg. Layer height 0.2 mm (0.16 mm optional for a finer seat). Walls: 4 perimeters; infill 100% (small part; guarantees deterministic balance and anti-crack margin around the seat). Brim 5 mm (five bulb islands and hub have modest individual footprints), clean bed + IPA, first layer ~0.2 mm at 30-50% speed. Slicer elephant-foot compensation on, plus the modeled 0.5 mm chamfers. Material: PLA (stiffer, seat Ø21.90) or PETG (tougher, seat Ø21.80, 0.10 mm/side interference). Print the included Ø21.90 test ring first — printed holes come out undersized and calibration-dependent. Assembly: drop nuts into pockets (13.25 mm pocket = +0.25 mm over AF, light press; dab of CA optional), press 608 with a vise against the outer race until it hits the shoulder, press grip caps into the bore. Optional sealed-nut variant: pause-at-height at 6.7 mm... i.e. at the pocket ceiling and let top layers bridge the 13.25 mm hex. Caps and body print together; ~3 h total.

**Why Fun:** It looks like it is already spinning — the constant 63-degree pitch of five spiral arms is the classic "frozen vortex" cue, so it invites a flick. Spun with the sweep (CCW from above) it visually "unwinds"; spun against, it "winds up" — a direction-dependent aesthetic no straight-arm spinner has. The engraved spiral grooves produce the documented expand/contract spiral illusion and a brief motion aftereffect when you stop and stare at it. Five bulbs mean a flickable, catchable lobe every 72 degrees (finer-grained than a tri's 120), and ~2.8e-5 kg*m^2 of steel-loaded inertia gives a strong gyroscopic push-back and multi-minute spins on a clean, dry 608.

**Why Unique:** Per the research pack, log-spiral arm spinners are nearly absent (one 2017 Fibonacci tri is the only precedent) and odd rotational symmetry (5-fold) is genuinely rare in a market that is half tri-arm clones — this design stacks both. It adds the spiral-disc optical illusion as an engraving rather than a sticker, and unlike commercial spiral-look novelty spinners it is engineered: tangent-blended constant-pitch spirals, steel mass at 79% of max radius, outer-race-only shoulder, and printed R188-style dished grip caps on a 608 platform. Nothing in the Torqbar/bar lineage or the tri/planetary print-in-place clusters resembles it.

**Risks:** (1) Bearing seat fit is calibration-dependent: too tight cracks brittle PLA around the bore (mitigated by 4 walls, 100% infill, lead-in chamfer, vise press, and the mandatory test ring); too loose spins the outer race in the seat (fix with a layer of tape or CA shim). (2) Sealed-nut variant bridges 13.25 mm over the hex — above the clean 10 mm limit — so expect underside sag; the open-top press-fit variant avoids this. (3) Nut mass variance or a missing nut unbalances the rotor and causes buzz/wobble; require five same-batch nuts. (4) Trailing-edge cusps curl on the first layer if the R2 fillet is omitted or brim is skipped. (5) Printed grip caps ride on the inner race; if the Ø11 contact ring is printed oversize it can rub the shield — verify free spin after pressing, and sand the ring if it drags. (6) 72 mm OD is above the 60-65 mm pocket-snag threshold from the research — this is a desk/EDC-pouch spinner, not a jeans-pocket one.


## Design 6: The Governor

**Direction:** Variable geometry: sliding or hinged masses that move outward under centrifugal force, changing inertia and sound mid-spin. Must be print-in-place or trivially assembled.

**Concept:** A closed-rim "flying saucer" spinner built around a centrifugal governor: three radial print-in-place sliders, each carrying a captive M8 steel nut, park near the hub behind small detents. Flick it up easily at low inertia, and past ~5 rev/s the sliders snap outward in unison with a triple clack — moment of inertia jumps ~25%, the spin note audibly drops, and the gyroscopic feel suddenly stiffens. As the spin dies the masses rattle back inward past the detents, giving a mechanical "death rattle" that announces the end of the spin.

**Geometry:** Overall: 88 mm OD x 12 mm thick disc, 3-fold rotational symmetry, flat bottom, closed outer rim (no arms). Top outer edge: 2 mm x 45-degree chamfer; all bottom edges: 0.5 mm x 45-degree chamfer (elephant-foot relief). BODY: solid disc profile; outer rim wall 2 mm thick (rim zone r=42 to r=44 solid via perimeters). HUB: central boss; bearing pocket from top face, bore diameter 21.85 mm (608 bearing, 22 mm OD x 8 mm ID x 7 mm; 0.075 mm/side interference for PLA — print a test ring first), depth 7.2 mm, with 0.5 mm x 45-degree lead-in chamfer at the pocket mouth; below the pocket a 19.0 mm diameter through-hole leaves a 4.8 mm thick retaining shoulder (the shoulder's flat annular ceiling is a 1.4 mm circular bridge — printable). CHANNELS: three radial T-slot channels at 120 degrees, radial extent r=14 to r=42 (outer end closed by the integral rim wall). Cross-section: channel floor at z=2.0 mm; base slot 20.0 mm wide x 7.0 mm tall; top opening (through top face) 15.4 mm wide, lip thickness 3.0 mm (z=9 to z=12), so each lip overhangs 2.3 mm per side, printed as a short bridge over the slider. SLIDERS (x3, printed in place): base block 19.3 mm wide x 6.6 mm tall x 18 mm long (0.35 mm lateral and 0.3 mm vertical clearance to channel); upper tower 14.7 mm wide x 3.1 mm tall passing through the top opening, top face 0.3 mm below the body top. Hex pocket in slider top: 12.9 mm across-flats (0.1 mm interference on a 13.0 mm AF M8 nut), 6.9 mm deep, 0.4 mm chamfer lead-in; nut pressed in flush by thumb after printing. Slider travel: 9 mm (inner parked position to outer stop); slider CoM moves from r=24 to r=33 mm. DETENTS: at the parked position, two 0.3 mm high x 2 mm long half-round ridges on the channel side walls engage matching notches in the slider flanks; release at roughly 4-6 rev/s. GRIP BUTTONS (x2, printed separately): 20 mm diameter dished caps, 2.5 mm rim thickness, concave top (3 mm deep dish, edges rounded R2); each has an 8.05 mm diameter x 3.4 mm long peg press-fitting the bearing bore from each side, with a 12 mm diameter x 0.5 mm tall boss so the cap bears only on the inner race and clears the outer race and shields. Parameterize in Fusion 360 as: OD, thickness, channel count (3), channel width, slider clearance, bearing bore, detent height — all driven from user parameters.

**Mass Distribution:** Total ~75 g: body ~40 g PLA (rim-biased via 5 perimeters and solid outer rim), 608 bearing ~12 g at center (negligible I contribution), 3 sliders ~2.5 g each plus 3 M8 nuts ~4.7 g each = ~21.6 g of moving mass, buttons ~3 g. Estimated body I ~35,000 g.mm2. Sliders parked (CoM r=24): I_total ~47,000 g.mm2; deployed (CoM r=33): ~58,500 g.mm2 — a ~25% inertia step, so angular velocity drops ~20% at deployment (angular momentum conserved) and spin-down lengthens because the same friction torque bleeds a larger I. Balance: all three channels, sliders, and nuts are identical and equally spaced; CoM stays on the bearing axis in both the parked state (detents define a repeatable position) and the deployed state (all sliders pinned against identical outer stops). Transient asymmetric deployment causes a brief buzz, then self-symmetrizes — part of the tactile character. Deterministic slicer infill on identical 120-degree sectors preserves balance.

**Print Plan:** Orientation: flat bottom on the build plate, no supports anywhere. The only overhangs are 45-degree chamfers (safe limit), the 1.4 mm bearing-shoulder ring bridge, and the 2.3 mm T-slot lip bridges landing over the in-place sliders — all well under the 5-10 mm clean-bridge threshold. Material: PLA primary (clearances as specced); for PETG add 0.1 mm to all sliding clearances (0.45 mm lateral, 0.4 mm vertical) since PETG fuses at PLA clearances. Settings: 0.2 mm layers, 0.4 mm nozzle, 4-5 perimeters, 40-50% infill, plus a slicer modifier volume at 100% infill within 3 mm of the bearing bore; first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed with a 5 mm brim; enable elephant-foot compensation. After printing: free each slider with a firm push (breaks the 0.3 mm first-layer tack), press one M8 nut into each slider by thumb, press the 608 bearing into the seat with a vise or clamp (never a hammer), press the two grip buttons into the bore. Print a 21.85 mm test ring first to calibrate the bearing fit. Assembly total: 3 nuts, 1 bearing, 2 buttons — under two minutes, no tools beyond a clamp.

**Why Fun:** It has states, and it tells you about them. Spin-up is light and quick (low parked inertia), then the spinner audibly shifts gear — three synchronized clacks and a pitch drop — and suddenly pushes back harder against tilt as L = I-omega jumps. Tilting it mid-spin gives the enhanced gyroscopic precession of a much heavier spinner without the heavy flick. The spin-down rattle is a built-in timer chime: you hear exactly when it is done. Between spins it doubles as a shake fidget — tilt it vertical and the sliders clunk back to their detents.

**Why Unique:** Variable-inertia sliding-mass spinners are patented commercially (US10525366) but almost absent from Printables/Thingiverse/MakerWorld, where the market is roughly half tri-arm clones plus print-in-place planetary gears (now table stakes). The Governor combines three underused elements from the research: a mechanism-driven inertia change (figure-skater effect in reverse), reliable impact-based sound design (clacks and rattle, not the failed whistle route), and an armless closed-rim saucer silhouette that reads nothing like a tri-arm or Torqbar-style bar. No published printable design couples detent-gated centrifugal sliders to an audible state change.

**Risks:** 1) PIP slider fusing: the T-slot lip bridges over the slider flange with only a 0.3 mm gap; poor cooling or over-extrusion welds them. Mitigation: clearances at the proven PLA sweet spot, short 2.3 mm bridges, and a documented fallback of printing sliders separately and inserting them before pressing the outer rim... (rim is integral, so fallback requires slicing sliders as separate parts and dropping them in through the top opening before nut insertion — the tower fits through the 15.4 mm opening only if the slider is tilted; verify in CAD or widen opening 0.5 mm in the fallback variant). 2) Detent wear: 0.3 mm PLA ridges will polish down over hundreds of cycles, lowering the release threshold; acceptable degradation, and detent height is a user parameter. 3) Nut pocket cracking: PLA is brittle at 0.1 mm hex interference — chamfered lead-in and 1.6 mm minimum pocket walls specified; PETG variant is more forgiving. 4) Bearing seat: printed bores come out undersized and vary by machine; the test-ring step is mandatory, and over-tight pressing can crack the hub (vise only, 100% infill zone specified). 5) Transient imbalance buzz if one detent releases late; detents are small precisely so release spread is narrow. 6) At 88 mm OD the spinner is at the large end of the comfortable range and will not pocket like a 52 mm EDC bar — it is a desk-class spinner by design.


## Design 7: Gyre-70

**Direction:** TPMS/gyroid lattice body: a rim-weighted spinner whose body is a printable gyroid/lattice shell — light center, dense rim, striking see-through look.

**Concept:** A ring-archetype spinner (the shape both world-record spinners used) whose entire mid-body is a see-through, cylindrically-mapped gyroid lattice: a light TPMS web connects a small solid hub to a heavy sealed rim carrying twelve captive 8 mm steel balls. It reads as a floating lattice halo — dense metal-weighted rim, nearly empty center — and the gyroid is modeled geometry, not slicer infill, so the light passes straight through it.

**Geometry:** Overall: annular disc, OD 70.0 mm, uniform height 12.0 mm, flat top and bottom faces (pure 2D-profile extrusion plus lattice). Three concentric zones, all parametric:

1. HUB RING (solid): OD 30.0 mm, height 12.0 mm. 608 bearing seat (22 x 8 x 7 mm): through-bore with seat diameter 21.90 mm for PLA / 21.80 mm for PETG (0.05 / 0.10 mm per-side interference), seat spans z = 2.5 to 9.5 mm so the bearing sits centered and recessed 2.5 mm from each face. Above the seat: counterbore D23.0 mm from top face to z = 9.5. Below: counterbore D23.0 mm from bottom face to z = 2.5, joined to the seat by 45-degree transition chamfers (no flat internal shoulders). 0.5 x 45-degree lead-in chamfer at the seat entry; 0.5 x 45-degree chamfer on all bottom outer edges (elephant-foot).

2. GYROID ANNULUS (lattice): radial span r = 15.0 to 25.0 mm, height 12.0 mm. Gyroid implicit field evaluated in cylindrical coordinates for exact 12-fold rotational symmetry: F = sin(X)cos(Y) + sin(Y)cos(Z) + sin(Z)cos(X) with X = 2*pi*(r - 15)/8.0, Y = 12*theta, Z = 2*pi*z/8.0 (8 mm radial/vertical cell, 12 angular repeats; arc-length cell ~10.5 mm at mid-radius). Shell the F = 0 isosurface to 1.2 mm wall thickness (offset +/-0.6 mm), trim to the annulus, boolean-union into hub and rim with 0.6 mm radial overlap on both interfaces. In Fusion 360: either Volumetric Lattice (Product Design Extension, gyroid, 8 mm cell, 1.2 mm thickness) applied to the annular body, or generate the exact-symmetry version as a mesh via script (numpy implicit field + marching cubes at 0.15 mm voxel), import, convert, and union.

3. RIM RING (solid): ID 50.0 mm, OD 70.0 mm, height 12.0 mm. Twelve sealed ball pockets: D8.3 mm x 8.4 mm deep vertical cylinders, floors at z = 1.6 mm, centers at r = 30.0 mm, every 30 degrees starting at 15 degrees. Twelve grip scallops on the outer face: concave R3.0 mm cuts, 1.0 mm deep, full height, every 30 degrees starting at 0 degrees (offset 15 degrees from pockets so minimum pocket-to-outer wall stays 0.85 mm). 1.0 mm 45-degree chamfers on top and bottom rim edges.

OPTIONAL GRIP CAPS (2x, separate prints): D20.0 mm disc, 3.0 mm thick with 1.5 mm concave dish, central stem D8.00 x 3.4 mm with 0.3 mm entry chamfer, push-fit into the 608 inner race from each side.

**Mass Distribution:** Deliberate barbell profile: twelve 8 mm chrome-steel balls (2.1 g each, 25.1 g total) at r = 30 mm plus a ~19.5 g solid PETG/PLA rim (mean r ~30 mm) put roughly 45 g of the ~67 g total at r > 25 mm; the gyroid web is only ~3.4 g (~18% relative density) and the hub + bearing (~17 g) sit at small radius where they cost little. Estimated I ~ 4.5e-5 kg*m^2 — several times a solid-body printed spinner of equal weight. Balance is guaranteed analytically: the cylindrically-mapped gyroid has exact 12-fold rotational symmetry (unlike Cartesian gyroid infill), pockets and scallops share the same 12-fold pattern, and identical balls in identical pockets keep the CoM on the bearing axis. Total ~67 g and 70 mm OD lands in the documented 60-70 g / 50-90 mm hand-feel sweet spot.

**Print Plan:** Material: PETG preferred (tougher 1.2 mm lattice walls, tolerates the 0.10 mm/side press fit; use 21.90 mm seat if PLA). Orientation: flat face down — the part is a flat-bottomed extrusion; hub, rim, and gyroid slice-curves all touch the plate. No supports: gyroid at 8 mm cell size is self-supporting (local overhangs stay near the 45-degree rule), internal seat shoulders are replaced by 45-degree chamfers, and pocket ceilings bridge only 8.3 mm (within the 10 mm clean-bridge limit). Settings: 0.2 mm layers (0.16 mm optional for the seat zone), 4 perimeters, 100% infill (all solid regions are thin rings anyway), 5 mm brim, first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed, seam position random (or aligned into one scallop) so seam mass cannot bias balance. G-code pause at z = 10.0 mm to drop the twelve balls (ball tops at z = 9.6 mm, 0.4 mm clearance under the bridged ceiling). Print a 5 mm-tall bearing-seat test ring first to calibrate the 21.8/21.9 mm bore; press the bearing with a vise, never a hammer. Caps print dish-down, no supports.

**Why Fun:** It looks like it shouldn't work — a heavy polished rim apparently floating on a translucent mathematical foam — and then it spins like the record-holders because it shares their physics (all mass at max radius, light middle). Light plays through the moving lattice while spinning, giving a shimmering depth effect no solid spinner has. The steel-ball rim gives strong gyroscopic push-back when tilted, the twelve scallops give a flickable edge every 30 degrees (finer than any tri-arm's 120), and the ring form catches and brakes cleanly between finger and thumb.

**Why Unique:** Research shows gyroid/TPMS appears only in trivial keychain spinners — a structural gyroid-shell rotor is essentially unexplored territory. This design goes further than dropping infill into a body: the gyroid is evaluated in cylindrical coordinates (Y = 12*theta) so the lattice itself has exact 12-fold rotational symmetry, solving the balance problem that makes Cartesian lattice bodies wobble — an engineering detail absent from every existing printed spinner. It is also a ring archetype (the proven record-spinner topology) rather than the tri-arm/bar forms that dominate ~half the market, and it hides its ballast: the steel balls are sealed invisibly inside the rim, so the mass source is a mystery.

**Risks:** (1) Gyroid wall slicing: 1.2 mm walls must slice as 2-3 clean extrusions; below ~1.0 mm effective width some slicers produce gap-fill stutter — verify in preview and thicken to 1.6 mm if needed (costs a little transparency). (2) Local gyroid overhangs near saddle points can exceed 45 degrees briefly; poor cooling shows as rim-side stringing — needs part-cooling fan at 100% above z = 2 mm. (3) Pocket outer wall is 0.85 mm at its thinnest; a hard drop or over-tight ball could crack it — balls are slip-fit (8.3 mm pocket), not pressed. (4) PLA seat cracking if the 21.9 mm bore prints undersized — the test ring is mandatory, and the lead-in chamfer matters more than bore tweaks. (5) Missed pause = balls not inserted and a 25 g lighter, dead-feeling spinner; the pause must be verified in G-code before starting. (6) If the Fusion PD Extension lattice (Cartesian gyroid) is used instead of the scripted cylindrical-mapped mesh, exact symmetry is lost — residual imbalance should still be small (~3 g lattice total) but may be perceptible; the scripted route is the correct one. (7) Ball rattle if pockets print tall — keep pocket depth 8.4 mm so the bridged ceiling lightly cages each ball; a drop of glue at insertion silences any residue rattle.


## Design 8: Cicada

**Direction:** Sound-designed spinner: geometry deliberately shaped to produce a tuned hum/whistle or clicky tactile detents (magnetless, purely geometric).

**Concept:** Cicada is a ring-silhouette spinner whose stator caps carry two printed leaf-spring pawls riding a fine-pitch internal ratchet crown on the rotor. At flick speed the ~2 kHz click train fuses into a buzzing tone that glissandos downward as the spin decays, ending in slow discrete ticks — a fully geometric, magnetless sound design that follows the research finding that per-rotation clicking (not air whistling) is the only acoustic mechanism that works at hand-spin RPM. A hollow annular rim cavity with five ports acts as a soundbox tuned near the click band, and a plain silent cap swaps in when quiet is wanted.

**Geometry:** OVERALL: full ring rotor, OD 68 mm, height 12 mm, flat bottom, all vertical walls (2D-profile extrusion plus one raised ring), no supports. Fusion 360 user parameters: OD=68, H=12, rim_t=9 (radial rim thickness, rim from Ø50 to Ø68), N_teeth=48, tooth_depth=0.6, pawl_tip_gap=-0.3 (interference), bore=21.8.

ROTOR (one part): (1) Outer rim: annulus Ø50-Ø68 x 12 mm containing an internal annular sound cavity Ø52-Ø66 x 8 mm tall starting 2 mm above the floor (roof bridges a 7 mm radial span with 45-degree chamfered top corners — within the 5-10 mm safe bridge limit); five Ø6 mm radial port holes through the outer face at 72-degree spacing, centered at mid-height (vertical-wall holes, printable). (2) Hub: Ø30 mm boss, 12 mm tall, with 608 bearing seat bored Ø21.8 mm x 7.2 mm deep from the top (0.1 mm/side PETG interference on the 22 mm OD), 0.5 x 45-degree lead-in chamfer, floor lip Ø19 mm so only the outer race seats; >=4 perimeters around the bore. (3) Five straight spokes 4 mm wide x 8 mm tall joining hub to rim (5-fold symmetry, coprime with 48 teeth to avoid beat modulation; also visually rare per research). (4) Ratchet crown: a raised ring on the hub top face, Ø36 outer / Ø30 inner x 3.5 mm tall, with 48 symmetric triangular teeth cut into its INNER face (tip circle Ø30.0, root circle Ø31.2, tooth depth 0.6 mm, circular pitch 1.96 mm, 90-degree included angle, 0.2 mm tip radius). Circular-pattern one tooth profile 48x.

CAP ASSEMBLY (stator): Bottom cap: Ø20 mm dished button, 3 mm skirt, with a Ø7.8 mm x 7.5 mm stub that passes through the 608's 8 mm bore. Top cap (the sound cap): Ø20 mm dished button whose underside has a Ø7.9 mm socket press-fitting the stub (clamping only the inner race), plus a Ø28.5 mm skirt from which two cantilever pawl blades emerge in-plane, 180 degrees apart but offset by half a tooth pitch (giving 96 clicks/rev): each blade 8.0 mm long x 0.8 mm thick x 3.0 mm tall, gently curved trailing shape, tip radius 0.4 mm, tip circle Ø30.6 mm (0.3 mm engagement into the 0.6 mm teeth). Blades flex in the XY print plane so bending stress runs along, not across, layers. A second, plain top cap (no blades, skirt Ø28 mm clearing the crown) is the silent-mode swap. All bottom edges get 0.5 x 45-degree chamfers for elephant's foot.

ACOUSTIC TUNING: cavity volume ~10.4 cm^3, five Ø6 x 3 mm ports -> Helmholtz resonance ~2.2 kHz, matching the 96 clicks/rev x ~20 rev/s = ~1.9 kHz onset click rate; pitch parameter is N_teeth (24/48/96 variants print from the same parametric model).

**Mass Distribution:** Ring archetype: essentially all printed mass sits in the Ø50-68 mm rim (I = mr^2 for a rim vs 0.5mr^2 for a disc — the record-holder topology). Rim walls print effectively solid via 6 perimeters around both cavity faces; the cavity that provides the soundbox also removes only mid-radius material, keeping the outermost 1.5 mm shell and the rim floor/roof heavy. Estimated masses: rim ~24 g, spokes+hub ~8 g, crown ~2 g, caps ~6 g, 608 bearing ~12 g; total ~52 g at 68 mm — inside the 60-70 g hand-feel band once optional weights are added. Optional: five 8 mm steel BB pockets (Ø8.2 mm) in the rim floor between ports, sealed by the cavity roof via pause-at-height, adding ~10 g at r=29 mm. Balance is guaranteed by strict 5-fold (rim, ports, spokes) and 48-fold (teeth) rotational symmetry with deterministic slicer infill; the two pawls are on the stator so their drag is symmetric side-load-cancelling and never unbalances the rotor. CoM stays on the bearing axis by construction.

**Print Plan:** Material: PETG strongly preferred (pawl blades are flexures — PLA fatigues and cracks at 0.3 mm repeated deflection; PETG also tolerates the 0.1 mm/side bearing interference). Rotor: flat bottom on bed, no supports anywhere — cavity roof is a 7 mm annular bridge with chamfered corners, ports and teeth are vertical walls, crown is a straight extrusion. 0.2 mm layers (0.16 mm for crisper teeth), 6 perimeters, 40% gyroid infill (perimeter count, not infill, carries the rim mass), brim optional. Caps: print button-face down, 100% infill, brim on (small footprint), first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed. Tolerances: bearing seat 21.8 mm with 0.5 mm lead-in chamfer, press with a vise; print the included 5-minute test ring first to calibrate the seat. Stub/socket 7.8/7.9 mm press pair. Pawl tip interference 0.3 mm is a parameter — print the sound cap at 0.2/0.3/0.4 to tune loudness vs drag (10 min, 2 g each). Total plate time ~2.5 h, ~35 g filament.

**Why Fun:** The spinner plays its own spin-down: a hard flick starts a bright ~2 kHz cicada buzz that slides continuously down in pitch as angular velocity decays, decomposing into individual freehub-style ticks in the last seconds — you hear the exponential decay curve. The 96 clicks/rev also give a fine-grained tactile grain through the fingers, and slow deliberate rotation works as a satisfying ratchet-clicker fidget in its own right. Swapping to the plain cap converts it to a smooth silent ring spinner, so it is two fidgets in one.

**Why Unique:** Commercial and printed spinners are overwhelmingly silent tri-arms/bars; the only documented sound attempts are whistles, which provably fail at hand RPM (the Cults "non-whistling spinner"). Cicada takes the research-validated click route but pushes it from novelty clicker to tuned instrument: tooth count as a pitch parameter, half-pitch dual pawls doubling frequency into the tonal fusion range, and a ported Helmholtz rim cavity as an integrated soundbox — no published spinner couples a ratchet source to a resonant rim chamber. It is also a ring silhouette with 5-fold symmetry, both individually rare in the tri-arm-dominated catalogs, and the sound is user-selectable via swap caps.

**Risks:** (1) Pawl drag shortens spin: two blades at 0.3 mm engagement cost real torque — buzz-mode spins will run tens of seconds, not minutes; mitigated by the printable interference sweep and the silent cap for long spins. (2) Flexure fatigue or creep: PLA blades will crack; even PETG blades left parked on a tooth tip may take a set — park the spinner in silent cap or note blades are a 10-minute reprint. (3) Concentricity: pawl tip circle rides on cap-to-bore registration; if the stub/socket fit is sloppy the engagement varies per revolution (warbling volume) — keep the stub press snug and test-ring the bearing seat. (4) Cavity roof bridge sag on poorly cooled printers could shed strings into the cavity and rattle — chamfered corners and <=7 mm span mitigate; a rattle is audible but harmless. (5) Helmholtz tuning is an estimate; real resonance may sit off the click band — port diameter is a parameter, and mistuning only flattens the timbre, never silences it. (6) Sub-millimeter tooth tips (0.2 mm radius) may print rounded at 0.2 mm layers, softening the click; drop to 0.16 mm layers or scale N_teeth down to 24 for a sharper, louder, lower-pitch variant.


## Design 9: Counterfeit Comet

**Direction:** Anti-spinner: a deliberately asymmetric-LOOKING but perfectly balanced design (asymmetric visual mass, hidden counterweights) that appears like it should not spin true but does.

**Concept:** A deliberately lopsided "comet" spinner: one massive teardrop head on one side, a wispy 5 mm-thin knotted tail trailing off the other, no rotational symmetry anywhere. It looks like it should buzz and wobble violently — but three hidden M8 steel nuts (one in the head, two disguised as knots in the tail) are placed so the center of mass sits exactly on the 608 bearing axis, and it spins dead true. The design weaponizes the viewer's intuition about balance: the fun is handing it to someone who refuses to believe it will spin.

**Geometry:** Envelope: 84 mm max diameter (45 mm max radius at head, 37 mm at tail), two Z-heights: main body 9.0 mm, tail blade 5.0 mm, all flat-bottomed on Z0. Origin = bearing axis. All bottom edges get 0.5 mm x 45 deg chamfers (elephant-foot control).

HUB: boss ø30 mm, 9.0 mm tall. Through-bore bearing seat for 608 (22 x 8 x 7 mm): seat ø21.85 mm for PLA (0.075 mm/side interference), ø21.80 mm for PETG; seat depth 7.0 mm centered in the 9 mm body (1.0 mm retaining lip each side, lip ID ø19.5 mm); 0.5 mm x 45 deg lead-in chamfer both faces; minimum 4 perimeters around bore. Two printed grip buttons: ø28 mm cap, 20 mm concave dish (1.5 mm deep), ø8.10 mm x 4.0 mm chamfered stub press-fit into the 8 mm inner race.

HEAD LOBE (theta = 0 deg): circle R18 mm centered at (24, 0), full 9 mm thick, blended to hub with tangent arcs — leading blend fillet R10, trailing blend R30 (gives the teardrop/comet look). Hidden pocket 1 inside head at radius r_head (parameter, initial 26 mm, tuning range 20-30 mm) on theta = 0.

TAIL: swept blade, centerline = arc of radius 30 mm about origin from theta 130 deg to 250 deg, width tapering linearly 14 mm -> 5 mm, thickness 5.0 mm (bottom flush with body; the step faces up, so no overhang). Two pod bulges ("knots"): ø18 mm bosses, 9.0 mm thick, centered on the tail centerline at theta 170 deg and 210 deg, radius r_tail (parameter, initial 28 mm, range 24-32 mm).

NUT POCKETS (3 total: head pocket + both pods): hex pocket across-flats 13.3 mm (M8 nut AF 13.0 + 0.3 clearance), depth 7.0 mm, floor 1.2 mm, roof 1.0 mm, internal corner radii 0.5 mm (crack-starter control). Pockets open upward, sealed by print pause.

FINE-TRIM RING: eight blind pockets ø4.6 mm x 3.0 mm deep, opening on the underside, at r = 22 mm every 45 deg — accept 4.5 mm steel BBs (~0.4 g) glued in after a static balance check; they read as a decorative dot ring.

BALANCE NULLING (Fusion 360): model body as one solid (PLA 1.24 g/cm3 or PETG 1.27), model three nut bodies as steel 7.85 g/cm3 in their pockets, include bearing as a 12 g ring body. Use Properties > Center of Mass; drive parameters r_head and r_tail until CoM XY offset from origin < 0.05 mm. These two radii give roughly +/-60 g-mm of tuning authority without altering the silhouette. Moment budget at initial values: tail side (2 nuts at 28 mm = 284 g-mm, tail plastic ~113, pods ~84, total ~480 g-mm at ~190 deg) vs head side (head plastic ~296 + head nut ~140 = ~436 g-mm at 0 deg) — inside tuning range.

**Mass Distribution:** Total ~60 g: printed body ~25 g (100% infill mandatory — infill must be deterministic or the CAD balance is void), 3x M8 steel nuts 16.2 g, 608 bearing ~12 g, buttons ~4 g, BB trim as needed. All three nuts sit at 26-28 mm radius (74-80% of max radius), so despite the asymmetric look the steel does classic rim-weighting work: estimated I ~ 26,000 g-mm2 (nuts ~12,000, head plastic ~7,400, tail+pods ~6,000), comparable to a good 68 g tri-spinner. Balance is achieved analytically in CAD (first-moment vector nulled to <0.05 mm CoM offset via the r_head/r_tail parameters), then verified physically: hold the axis horizontal, let the heavy side settle downward, and glue a 4.5 mm BB into the trim pocket opposite; repeat until it holds any position. This two-stage approach (CAD nulling + BB trim) absorbs nut mass variance and printer-to-printer extrusion differences.

**Print Plan:** Orientation: flat bottom on plate, zero supports — every feature is a vertical wall, an upward-facing step (9 mm body vs 5 mm tail), or an up-opening pocket. Material: PETG preferred (tail-blade toughness, more forgiving press fit at 0.10 mm/side); PLA acceptable at 0.05 mm/side seat interference. Layer height 0.2 mm; first layer 0.2 mm at 30-50% speed with a brim (small-ish footprint, thin tail). Walls: 4 perimeters; infill 100% (non-negotiable — balance depends on deterministic mass). Pause-at-height at Z = 8.0 mm: insert the three M8 nuts (drop-in, 0.3 mm clearance, no force), resume; the 1.0 mm roof prints directly onto the steel nut tops so bridging is only the 0.15 mm clearance ring. Bearing: press in with a vise using the 0.5 mm lead-in chamfers; print a single-wall test ring of the seat first (printed holes come out undersized and vary per machine). Buttons press into the inner race by hand. Run the bearing dry and open; clean with IPA. Approx 45-60 g filament, ~3 h.

**Why Fun:** The entire toy is a physics prank. Everyone who sees it predicts violent wobble; flicking it and watching it run glass-smooth produces a genuine double-take, and explaining why (CoM on axis is all that matters, I = sum of m*r^2 doesn't care about symmetry) makes it a conversation piece. As a fidget it still performs: the big head gives a deep 360-degree flick landmark, the thin tail edge gives a second lighter texture per revolution, and because the steel sits at 74-80% radius the gyroscopic push-back is strong for its 60 g. While spinning, the asymmetric silhouette blurs into a ghostly off-center ring — visually unsettling in a way symmetric spinners never are.

**Why Unique:** Research found no existing asymmetric-but-balanced spinner: the market is ~50% tri-arm clones plus bar/quad/ring variants, all strictly rotationally symmetric, and the underused-shapes survey explicitly flags visually-lopsided-but-statically-balanced rotors as unclaimed territory ("no hits found for spinners"). Commercial "weird asymmetric" novelty spinners exist but are actually unbalanced and spin badly. This design's differentiators: (1) engineered first-moment nulling with hidden counterweights as the core feature, not decoration; (2) dual-thickness comet silhouette impossible to mistake for a tri-arm at any distance; (3) a parametric balance workflow (two tuning radii + CoM readout + BB trim ring) that makes the trick reproducible on any printer, which is the hard part no novelty spinner solves.

**Risks:** 1) Residual imbalance: M8 nut mass varies +/-0.2 g and extrusion flow varies per printer — mitigated by CAD nulling to <0.05 mm plus the underside BB trim ring; without the trim step expect slight buzz. 2) Printing below 100% infill or with different wall counts silently breaks the CAD balance — must be stated in print instructions; sparse infill in an asymmetric body is the failure mode. 3) Missed pause-at-height leaves pockets empty: grossly unbalanced and the illusion inverts (it wobbles exactly as it looks); slicer pause must be verified before the print. 4) PLA seat cracking on press-fit if interference exceeds ~0.05 mm/side or the vise is skipped — chamfer plus test-ring protocol mitigates. 5) The 5 mm tail blade is the weakest section (drop impact); PETG and the 14 mm root width mitigate, and the R10/R30 blend fillets avoid stress risers at the hub. 6) Elephant's foot could tighten the bore first layers — 0.5 mm chamfer plus slicer compensation. 7) Hand feel: only one large flick lobe per revolution (like a 2-arm bar but singly), so repeated-flick cadence is slower than a tri-arm — inherent to the concept, partially offset by the tail edge as a secondary flick surface.
